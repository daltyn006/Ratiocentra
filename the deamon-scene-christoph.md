# Christoph: The Third Step

Christoph had been told, in the lonely and practiced voice of a woman who had said it four thousand times before, that the mind he was about to enter was his own. He had not believed her. He believed it now.

The room had no walls. This did not trouble him the way he had expected it to. There seemed to be a floor — grey, cool through the soles of feet he no longer had, or had never had, he could not decide which — and above it a ceiling of the same grey, close enough to touch, if he had reached, which he did not. Somewhere behind him he could hear water. He did not turn to look for it. He had learned, in the eight months of tests before this one, that looking for the water only moved it.

He had also learned that the deamon did not knock.

It was, at first, simply that the room contained one more shadow than it should have. Christoph had counted shadows before — an old habit from childhood, from a father who did not trust rooms that looked too honest — and this room, lit from a source he could not locate, had cast exactly none, until it cast one.

*You are in the third,* it said, or did not say, in the manner of a thought that arrives already agreed with.

He did not ask the third what. He had learned that, too.

The shadow did not move closer. It did not need to. Distance in the room worked the way weather worked — it simply arrived. Christoph found himself an arm's length from something that had no arm's length to be measured from, a shape assembled loosely from the idea of a man, the way a child draws a man before it has met enough men to know how they disagree with each other.

"I passed the second step," Christoph said. His voice came out echoing in the smallness of the ever-altering room, which unsettled him more than the shadow had. "They erased everything. I answered honestly."

*Honesty,* the shadow said, *is a door. It is not the room.*

He thought of his mother's hands, which he had not been permitted to remember. until the second step ended and gave his memories back to him one at a time, like a debt paid in installments. He thought of them now without meaning to, and the shadow noticed him thinking, the way a draft notices an open window.

*Give me the hands,* it said.

"No."

*Then show me something you would rather I had.*

This, Christoph understood — not with relief, but with the cold, arriving clarity of a man realizing the floor beneath a frozen lake is closer than he thought — was the actual test. Not whether he had a soul. Whether he would trade a stranger's for his own, given the chance, in a room with no one watching except the thing built to watch.

He thought of Anderson, three worlds away in the waking world, who had gone under three weeks before he had. The vision grating against the room like nails on a chalk board. Had he undergone the same experience? Would he even remember? Then, He thought of Grant, who was not here, who would never be here, because Grant did not believe any of this proved anything at all, and had said so, loudly, in the street, in front of people who hastily forced his identity down in their minds. Still, he cannot evade reality.

*Grant,* the shadow repeated, tasting it like it was new to language. *He is irrational. Show me him instead.*

"He's my friend..."

*I did not ask what he was to you. I asked for him.*

The water around him had grown louder. Seemingly out of view the whirling of the waves turned to a howling of a storm. He understood, distantly, that this meant something — that sound in a room with no walls was not weather but consequence, and that the third step measured how long a man could stand in rising water and still decline to hand someone else the rope. He did not know if this was true. He had decided, somewhere in the last several minutes or several months, that whether a thing was true and whether a thing was happening to him were no longer separate questions. Wintry wind sent sleek frigid pain throughout his abscent body, locking his perception on everywhere except the highly figure as the cold turned to a blasting furnace of embarassment, anxiety, and stress.

"Yes," he said, and this time his voice sank into the errily murky waters which grew to envelop him.

The shadow did not answer. It did not need to. It simply stopped being a shadow that had arrived, and became, instead, the ordinary absence of light that a room without walls is entitled to — and Christoph understood, the way one understands a held breath has ended only once it has, that the third step was already over, and had been for some time, and that he had spent its final measured moments arguing with a room that had already decided about him and was only waiting, out of some old machine courtesy, for him to finish being afraid. For him to let go. Bubbles appeared which he only could assume were his in the rapidly blackening tides which trailed slowly up, as the ceiling grew further away. Was he sinking? Was the roof rising? He had no clue. 

But there, somewhere above the ceiling that was not a ceiling, in a body he had almost stopped believing was his, something in his chest declined, very quietly, to continue.

The machine logged the reason. The machine always logged the reason. No one who could have used the information could ever read it.
