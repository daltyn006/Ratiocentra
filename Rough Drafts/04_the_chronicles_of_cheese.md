# Part 4: The Chronicles of Cheese

Very few should know this, but one of my very first novels was written
in fifth grade. This is my newest rendition of it, since I lost the
papers at my babysitter's house years ago. Although nothing could ever
beat the original, I think it is a fitting addition to this series. I
hope to shorten this true story while staying true to the story line.
Sadly, there will be fewer drawings in this book.

## Chapter One: The Gouda and the Bad

This was a society much like our own — yet many variations of cheese
ruled in its place. Very few knew of our protagonist. He was a select
type of gouda, which happened to be the second most common variety.
There were many different types, some superior to others, the rarest
being select aged cheddars and bries, often holding the highest
positions in government.

Our cheese — Les, we may call him — was nothing of the sort. He was a
young Dutch gouda of around three months, newly assigned to write what
was on his mind. He was not the brightest in the class of 1989. He was
on track to graduate a year after the average Muenster. Bitto cheeses
were very intelligent, often graduating in under a year, some even in
under two months. The average graduation advancement was about a year,
with processed single cheeses taking the longest — up to two.

Les often struggled in history, politics, and government. These subjects
barred him from the upper echelons of his class and brought him to work
at the printing office. No cheese was self-sufficient in society — upon
birth they were removed from their mold and introduced to school. Any
cheese Les asked on the street would reply with a simple "I don't know"
when he asked questions. His teachers did not enjoy his curiosity,
calling it un-cheese-like. His boss was the same, repeatedly giving him
detention for writing his questions alongside the documents he was
supposed to copy. Detention was the same as his work, except for each
incorrect word he would be put under a heat lamp for three minutes as
punishment.

## Chapter Two: Meeting Mozzarella

He saw her from across the train station. Her pale, slim figure had a
yellow tinge from the old electric lights. Les was never one to date
outside the system, but there was something about the way she rolled
that brought a primal attraction to mind. Gracefully wheeling over to
her, he began: "Hello, I'm Les. I'm turning a year old now and am
looking forward to settling down." After taking a good look at him, she
responded, "I remember you, idiot." Pausing for a laugh: "We shared a
government class together in our second month — I'd always laugh at your
questions. I'm Mar. Mar Mozzarella?" Les, not knowing his lefts from
rights, asked, "Would you like to go for milk at the local Dairy Queen
this Monday?" She paused and looked around, profiling those in earshot.
"Goat," she whispered. Not knowing what this odd woman was up to, he
whispered back, "Huh?" "Meet me instead at 100 Cheddar Lane — this is
me," she said, motioning to the oncoming train. And she was gone. So was
Les' mind, wondering what she wanted in the business district.

The few interesting moments between now and then were his transfer to
"creative writing," where he would invent whatever he wanted alongside
around fifty others in his building — a clear upgrade in status, as he
was one of ten writing about scientific advancements. Due to old
curiosity, he checked on his former workplace soon after the change. He
found each room now stocked with large towers, buttons blinking green,
an incessant whirring emanating from each. The room was nearly frozen,
dangerous for any wheel or stick. Les soon left, confused. He went to
the mail room — a familiar stop. It too was different: a foreign object
now printed papers. Rolling over for a look, Les was baffled by what was
on the page. It was his writing — but not his writing. The bold black
ink was blotched neatly along straight lines. "You shouldn't be in
here!" he heard from behind. He turned and saw a brick of Limburger.
"I'll escort you out, sonny." The brick said with finality.

## Chapter Three: A Brick of Brutality

After returning to his mundane days, Les kept wondering why his office
was needed. Sure, his writing would appear in schools and newsletters —
but he had never been influential on so grand a scale. It happened to be
Monday, the busy cheese's day off. Les rolled over to Cheddar Lane with
a new confidence; he was popular, after all. He saw the building marked
"100"; Mar was relaxing on a bench nearby. Her strands of what would, on
a human, be called hair blew in the wind. She looked even whiter in the
sun. But that image wouldn't last long — before he knew it, he was being
dragged hastily down the nearby alley and shoved into a hallway beneath
building 100. Les thought at first to call for police, but Mar assured
him: "Sorry, but we can't be seen." This comment deeply hit Les, and he
moved with the crowd that suddenly filled the hall. He really had no
option but to go with them, having been taught it was rude not to follow
a crowd.

The crowd filtered slowly into the auditorium. There was a whole array
of different cheeses — a wedge here, a stick there, bricks commonplace.
A moldy brick of old cheddar now commanded the group's attention. He
stood out from the rest because he was on the stage, which Les found
quite intriguing. His question was soon to be answered, as the brick
spoke: "I rather enjoy our time here, folks," he bellowed, "but it will
soon be over, for my time is growing short." Disappointment was tangible
in the crowd. "And with this I leave you with a question. Are you
happy?"

Les had never thought of this before — emotions rarely came to his mind
after sociology class. But the brick was not finished: "I hope that one
day all of you will find your own personalities. Your calling. Your
life. Mine is long over, but my progress is visible. All of you here now
are a product of questioning. My purpose was to bring you together,
forming this group. Now, we have amassed thousands in our subsociety."
Large banners unraveled from the walls depicting red and white stripes.
Thirteen wheels of parmesan were set in the top left corner in a circle.
"This flag bears our cause. Our thirteen divisions work tirelessly to
gain in numbers, for a purpose beyond that of our world." Les had never
seen anything like this before, the colors appearing only on the rarest
Blue Cheese or Leicester.

"I find myself saddened by our degree of hierarchy in society. Although
many do not recognize what is occurring — due to our ever-increasing
number of 'studies' and 'data' being produced — if this were to be
destroyed, maybe, just maybe, might we come to our senses and bring
freedom to this nation." The brick turned his back to the crowd and
ascended the risers, accompanied by applause. He turned back around; a
brigade of bricks, sticks, and wedges came to the front of the stage.
Mar was among them. "These young soldiers are preparing a strike on the
central terroir." This caught Les off-guard — not that someone had been
influenced to fight for a cause, that went over his head. But that Mar
was a soldier, a status much higher than his own. The room built to a
crescendo of applause — cheeses opening and closing their mouths — and
then suddenly it stopped.

## Chapter Four: Sharp Cheddar

Les was always curious to meet new types of cheese, finding all
varieties interesting. But a strong stench soared through the room
faster than homemade cheese. He had only known this scent once before,
in his birthplace. Any cheese factory was guarded by only the most
formidable cheese — it was their livelihood, after all. But now, the
whole assembly was surrounded by wheels upon wheels of Milbenkäse and
Casu Marzu. Some tried to flee and met a horrible fate. The rest held
their ground, then followed the orders given by these guards. One after
another, they were driven to the rehabilitation complex. Smoothly, the
guards put each prisoner in a cell for questioning. Now Les was still in
blissful ignorance. Having attended only the single meeting, he expected
a simple ousting.

Finally, it was his turn to be interrogated — but his path diverged from
the other wheels. He was escorted to a vehicle that took him far from
everything he had known. Deep in the forest there was a small community,
hidden in a valley beyond many mountains. It must have been at least two
days since he left the van. Upon exit, he was greeted with a long dirt
road stretching further than any normal wheel could roll. The wedge
driving the van wiggled behind Les and removed his restrictions. "Get a
move on, we need to go deeper," he said. "Into the woods now. On you
go."

The break from the city was quite nice for Les — he rarely caught time
to visit a park. The two were surprisingly agile, bounding over log and
leaf, leaping over ferns and wading through water. The wedge was an
excellent guide, giving clear directions whenever Les grew confused. And
finally, they arrived. A singular white pillar erupted miles into the
sky, its surroundings cleared of grub and stub. The grass was cleanly
cut; each bush trimmed. Entering the white gates of the building, the
two were met with a heavy glow from the lights above.

Very few rooms occupied the entrance — only a hallway and an elevator.
The white hallways were dim, lightly reflecting the moody lights on the
ceiling. Les decided now would be a good time to ask questions as he
rolled inside. "What am I doing here?" The guard responded: "I don't
know." "Where are you taking me?" The guard responded lazily: "Here." A
grand success. He was given answers to his questions. Les certainly had
more, but he was content with the information received. The elevator
slowly crawled up and up, stopping occasionally because the wedge had
accidentally hit extra buttons. Reaching floor six, the wedge shoved Les
out. The elevator closed behind him, and the wedge was off to another
floor. This floor had black granite lining the path, a single red light
shining menacingly down. Les, deciding this was fine, moved forward.

An astringent, creamy aroma befell the halls. The smell penetrated his
non-existent nose and forced its way into each of his pores. The hallway
was slowly expanding in height. Far in the distance, Les noticed a door
— a grandiose door, so large it had to be at least three levels tall. It
opened before him. This must be the congregation of the oldest cheddar
cheeses in the population. He had heard rumors of a government beyond
the government in school but dismissed them all as hearsay. Stage upon
stage was stacked to the roof. Now he found the rumors were true. Each
stage held a single cheddar ambassador of one specialty. The Chief
Assemblyman soon asked, "Chief Meeting-starter, would you like to start
this meeting?" The Chief Meeting-starter responded, "We shall start with
our Chief of Warfare." The Chief of Warfare declared, "I shall delegate
this to my pupil, the Executive Warfare Officer." This continued for
some time until, after the longest delay, they settled on a victim. The
lowly head of starting meetings was tasked with starting this one.

"Well, Les — you have been a great pain to the nation, and yet a model
citizen of what should be, except for one thing. Why do you ask so many
questions?" He asked. "Huh?" Les responded. The superior of the head of
meetings gestured, "Stop doing that! Why do you cheeses always rebel?"
"I don't want to rebel," Les claimed. But the cheddar legends were too
shrewd to be tricked. "We know you were at that rebellion." A couple of
higher-ups spoke, interrupting each other, and apologized in turn. This
rationale went on for quite some time, until the point of their summons
finally came clear. "We want more people to be as... unconventional as
you," the Chief Assemblyman acknowledged. "And we need to understand you
to produce more like you."

## Chapter Five: A Feta Unimaginable

A large news helicopter appeared in the background, visible only to Les.
Something felt off, so he gestured: "Hey — what's that doing there?" The
cheddar elites, accustomed to his ways, all turned. Panic broke out; the
General of Warfare slammed a large red button. An automated message
calmly instructed everyone to return to the bunkers at the bottom of the
elevator, as a nuclear bomb would be arriving shortly to neutralize any
threat. While the cheddars clambered down from their heights, Les made
for the elevator. Being first in line, he was first to wait patiently
for it. As it could hold only around ten, he and nine others descended.

Now Les was less than a hero, but he paused to think. If there was a
secret government behind the official government, why did they want
everyone to be incompetent? So he asked those in the elevator: "Why do
you want everyone to be incompetent?" "They are easier to help," a
random official said. "We live in a first-come, first-served society,
after all. It's only decent to help those who can't provide for
themselves." Les thought for a moment, then continued: "If they're
competent, couldn't they help themselves?" A clearly distinguished
executive responded: "If there was no one to help, how could we exist?
We are a group — our existence proves society is flawed and that you
need us!" Les thought again: "But... if you want us to be incompetent,
that makes us dependent on you. Wouldn't that mean you are making
society flawed?" Another officer said: "If we are making society flawed,
why do we call for equality?" Les was stumped. Then he thought of
something original: "If you people are all incompetent, and you want us
to also be incompetent — why are you not us?" The nine in the elevator
were stunned in silence, for they had no answer.

The long, tedious elevator ride was almost to the second floor. Les
remembered a simple summary of what the old brick had said: "I hope the
day comes that all of you find your personalities and create a calling."
With this new inspiration, he hit the button for the first floor.
Perhaps another cheese held a new life outside the confines of social
hierarchy and bias. The elevator crawled to a halt and shook under the
weight of ten cheeses. A final use came for his scholarly learning: it
was always rude not to follow when invited. "We should all go for a hike
outside," Les chuckled. "I invite you." None stayed in the elevator, and
it was too slow to make a second trip in a single day. A distant
whistling came from afar. With a flash of light, Les' story was over.
