# Part 3: My Old School

Lockers surrounded me; tiles covered everything except the ceiling. My
colleagues had warned me about teaching — having worked for only two
years, I found no issue. And still they claimed children found solace in
causing you pain. But I disagree. They were rowdy, yes, but their
intentions were good. I wanted to give back to society what it had given
me. My peers mostly warned me about one child: Grant. They said his
parents disappeared soon after he was born, with no explanation, leaving
him to his uncle. There was a predicament: the school board needed to
decide whether to assign him to me, or his uncle. This was the worst
part. His uncle was a teacher here, at New Mexico Elementary.

It was not that he was a bad person, per se, but his presence changed
each room he entered. The lights would start to dim as if adjusting to
him, and people talking at the same volume would seemingly quiet. He
refused to use any technology, calling it useless. Most found this
ironic since their computers always seemed to malfunction when he was
around. The tiles gave way to drywall as I entered the auditorium — our
school had never received enough funding to build a proper meeting room,
its money going instead into administrator after administrator who
decided what I could and couldn't teach. As I entered the meeting, I
noticed Bertha, the union representative, sitting in the back. She
rarely supported policies I agreed with, but was a useful ally when I
needed support.

"Shall we start this meeting?" Stacy asked rhetorically as I entered.
"Good, let's get started." Roll call was taken and the consent agenda
began. Most items were simple yes-or-no questions — whether to spend
more on football, or buy a furnace for the arts pottery program. But I
was only interested in what affected me directly. "Raise your hand for
Ryan; keep your hands down for Ethan," Stacy finally stated. I was eager
to see whether Grant would be assigned to me or Ethan. All hands shot
up, as if to say Grant was a bad influence. I was pleasantly curious to
see how he would actually be. With the meeting adjourned, I left for
home.

With dawn hitting my window, I awoke — a pleasant enjoyment lost in the
winter. The final days of summer were coming to a close, and the fall
semester was starting. Looking at the clock, I saw 7:30 AM. With a
fifteen-minute drive to campus, I was late. Rushing to prepare, I put on
my clothes, grabbed my briefcase, phone, keys, and wallet. Scurrying to
my car, I sped out of the driveway and fretted over the difficulties of
the first day. It was always a pain to enter through the metal detector
doors installed over the summer — the metal implant in my hip from a
childhood accident would always set them off. But today, walking
through, it failed to detect anything. "Stopped bringing in that
pocketknife?" Michael joked. "Finally made an exception for me?" "No —
there must be some sort of error," he rebutted. The gymnasium was packed
with children already; I was late enough that half my class was already
seated in a row, thanks to Allison. "Thanks for covering for me," I
whispered under the shouts of excited children. "I owe you one."
"Really? On the first day," she replied. "Did you decide to party last
night to celebrate?" "No, I made myself a sandwich and went to bed," I
answered. "My alarm didn't go off." I secretly checked my phone — fully
charged, no notifications.

"Get back to your class," Allison said. More had arrived while my back
was turned, sitting alongside their classmates. All but one. I deduced
this was Grant. His green eyes looked empty, dark in the gymnasium's
moody lighting, sunken beneath his brow. He wore a brown t-shirt and
blue shorts, obviously having dressed himself. His hair was a white,
curly blonde, creating paths of its own down his back — longer than was
typical for a second grader — tangled in strange braids. I began roll
call while inspecting the group. Stacy interrupted. "Alright everyone —
we're going to recite the pledge of allegiance, then head to class for
some fun!" The morning went quite well, though I noticed Grant was
uninterested in anything I taught, opting instead to look at random
corners of the room, as if something were there. Lunch was different.
Although the children were respectful to me in class, the lunchroom
changed them all upon entry. The only one who remained calm was Grant —
he was slowly gaining my respect. Returning to class, he again fixed his
gaze on the darkest corners of the room. "Grant, may I have your
attention please?" "I'm sorry," he whispered. His head snapped forward
but slowly drifted back toward either the window or the door. At recess,
the other children played while Grant sat alone on a bench facing the
woods. He seemed invisible to the rest, receiving only the occasional
whispered comment about his strangeness — but his quiet demeanor was
growing on me.

The weeks turned to months as spring erupted from every flower and leaf.
Grading my last papers before easing up on assignments, I reflected on
each student's average. Grant was surprisingly among the top grades.
Although he rarely watched the board as I wrote, he seemed to know
everything I had to teach. Now back in class, I found him behaving more
and more strangely — seeming to talk into those corners of the room,
though no sound came from his lips. The boys and girls began glancing at
him. It was as if he had only just appeared in the classroom for the
final few days. The day after lunch, everything felt off. Not wrong,
exactly — just empty. I ate my lunch in solace, relieved by the brief
respite from the children. Grant entered my classroom. But it was not
Grant. Grant usually dressed the way a child would who dressed
themselves — mismatched and bright. But now he wore black leather
loafers, pale stone trousers, and a white dress shirt. A silver overcoat
draped from his shoulders, matching his white-blonde hair. A
black-brimmed beret sat contrasting against it, and though he was still
only seven, he carried a beautiful wooden cane etched with gothic
designs.

"Would you like to hear a secret?" Grant said in his usual childish
voice. "No — but thank you anyway," I decided to reply. "I'll be
returning shortly," Grant said, and left. I figured he was right, as
lunch hour was almost done. The clock ticked to one, then two, then
three, and nobody entered. I ultimately decided to leave, as my day was
technically over, and stepped into the familiar hallway. Everything was
the same: the spotless floors covered in dust from children's shoes, the
slightly crooked art on the walls, each step to the exit — except for
one thing. Nobody was around. No sound of birds, no insects. Approaching
my car, I felt someone staring at me. I turned and saw Grant again, his
eyes now carrying a marginal red hue. "Are you ready this time?" His
cadence was slow and methodical, as if this question had been rehearsed.
"I know something that will bring you great joy and pleasure," he
continued. "It is with a great burden that I cannot share it with anyone
— but I cannot live my life without bringing this happiness to someone
else." "Where is everyone?" "They are gone now — do not fret. Right now,
all that matters is the secret. So would you like to hear it?" I studied
Grant. Nothing had really changed beyond the cosmetics. The past year he
may have been odd and seemingly troubled, but he had earned my respect
through his grades and quiet manner. "Yes," I concluded.

The world stood still. Empty as before, but now with a new desolation —
as if the whole universe had abandoned us two for this singular moment.
"There is a being out there, knowable only as God. I have found it and
have learned to become a part of it. Only very few have learned how to
assimilate into its love, but I think you may be one of them." What
happened next was incomprehensible. He said something without saying it
and disappeared. Then, on a whim, he reappeared directly in front of me.
"You could share in this power," he added slyly, "only by being willing
to learn in these ways." "In what ways?" I echoed. "Repeat my thoughts.
Remember that you are God — not the whole, but a piece — and that all
our pieces are trying to assemble something great." His voice distorted,
slowly deepening as though walking back and forth, but the pain was in
my mind. Grant noticed. "In other terms: remember that you, and
everything else, is an atom. All of these atoms accumulate into God. I
am just as valuable as what I consume; my energy is all contributed
toward God." My soul found no benevolence in his words, but my brain was
succumbing to the logic. I soon found myself repeating Grant. He spoke,
and I spoke. He moved, and I moved. He listened, and I listened. As if
in a trance, I copied each of his moves — and suddenly I was gone. Then,
just as fast, I was back again.

A great pain harmed my soul — twisting and bending it, torturing it like
something being slowly torn apart and atomized, until it cried out in
agony visible only behind my eyes. Not even to me. For my body had been
reborn; the energy around me shifted to match an exuberant pleasure that
overcome me. I had no comprehension of what had just occurred, but the
sheer pleasure of it was more addicting than any physical drug. I
instantly begged and pleaded for him to show me again, but I was met
only with a now motherly, sheepish "No."

After a while, when the immense happiness slowed to a reasonable smile,
I recovered my thoughts. "What just happened?" I asked, forgetting my
previous life. "And how can I do it again?" His intonation became
menacing: "I must warn you — do not fall into the depths of God for too
long. This has been our great family tradition for millennia. But in
light of my parents becoming the earth, I've decided to share this
pharmakon. The amount of knowledge I have found through God is greater
than any man will know." "How long until I can do that again?" I asked,
dismissing his seriousness like the words of a politician. "Give
yourself time to heal. My genetics have been accustomed to God. You are
still learning. You will be able to comprehend Him before the next
school year."

The next few months I met with Grant on occasion, learning his ways and
methods. His customs differed greatly from mine; he was able to commune
with some far-away being I could not yet perceive. Days blurred
together, and the pleasure that came with my transience grew elongated.
Grant remarked one afternoon on my astute performance. I did not care —
I only knew my body needed more.

Tonight Grant decided to meet later than usual. "Tonight is the night
you meet the entity knowable only as God." This time his words were
different, as if spoken from two mouths. A slight whistle pierced
everything surrounding him, and my ears began to soar. I, of course,
repeated this perfectly, driven for jubilation. Grant moved in a way not
humanly possible, yet it was done. I blissfully mirrored him, as if in a
frenzy. Then he stopped, and the world began to move. People who had
been gone for months returned — but I still did not care. If there were
people, or no people, it did not matter. The only thing that mattered
was meeting God. I knew this. And Grant knew this. I waited beside him,
listening to the hubbub around us — words spoken fleetingly by those
unknowing of the great blessing I had been given. I soared between
atoms, becoming electricity once more; a new superior consciousness rose
beyond my body.

I am in heaven. That was all that could be thought. No other possibility
presented itself. A great figure slowly and meaningfully approached —
but this was not God. This was Grant. Only it was not Grant. "Welcome to
omnipotence," he said. "Everything that has ever happened and will
happen is known here. But remember my warning: do not stay here long,
lest you become a part of this place." A great and loving darkness came
into the everlasting light shining into my energy. Grant spoke in
reverence: "This is your God." I stood stunned, incapable of movement.
My body was no longer my own, but now a part of this being. It spoke —
but didn't speak. It was understood between me and God that with any
religion, there is a purpose to go and spread the word. That is what I
would do.

As I returned to the earthly plane, I noticed a new bounty of people
around me, though they seemed to look right through me. My will was
determined: share this influence with whoever was interested. This would
bring me great pleasure, since the process itself had grown stale. I
recognized an unmistakable face — Allison. Her bright brown eyes fought
her sharp jawline; her long straight hair matched. The light freckles
distinct on her pale skin. Her petite frame now a fixation of mine — I
was sure to share my pleasures with her. As I approached, she stepped
back. I stepped back, and she stepped forward. Tired of this, I
continued in pursuit. "Do you want to know my secret?" Just as before, I
expected a simple 'No, thank you' — but she was all too intrigued, and
said after a pause, "Well, it's a free market." As she was an adult, it
was more difficult, but after some convincing it was just as before. We
were both entranced. I spoke, and she spoke. I moved, and she moved.
Soon enough, she was begging for more. But she was not my only target.

Soon enough school started, and I commenced with it. I stopped teaching
reality and began with the motions. As they were children, it was easier
to initiate them — but most were still hopeless. Some days I would come
to class and find a few missing, always those who had understood my
words, my motions, my thoughts. But I felt empty in my purpose,
incapable of introducing more and more into my life. I was given an
impossible task, and there was nothing I could do to fulfill it. Days
again turned to weeks; the leaves began to turn and fall. I tired of my
purpose, the failure too much to bear. This time, I turned to Grant. He
held no theory. I figured then — if I hold omnipotence, why not ask it
how to feel whole again? After completing the process I asked God, "What
can I do to please you? I just want to feel whole once more." It replied
in a single word: "Spread."

And so I did — but the pleasures that once felt good now turned to pain
and torment. My soul grew tired and hurt, blazed by a thousand suns.
Each time the process was completed, it took something away from me.
Over time my soul went numb, and my mind and body followed. Indifferent
to the fate I was sharing, I continued my path — this time with a new
purpose: find the pleasure I had once been given.

I returned to my school, bringing with me my pains, my torments, my
anguish, and my misery. This time God came to me, and I spoke: "If I
cannot change them to know your glory, I must take away their
suffering." My motions were beyond my own understanding; I became
darkness. Night befell the school's proximity as I entered. Great clouds
began a deluge too strong for any human to pass. Wind spun around the
school, twisting and bending the water in a circular motion. Trees,
grass, and cars were destroyed, but it served its purpose: each student
was contained in their room, and I knew this. Now a new entity in
itself, I roamed the halls. The lights stayed on but produced no light
under my influence. The tiles had a similar effect — distorted as if
underwater as I approached.

One classroom after another I conquered. It was not difficult; my new
flesh could transcend any physical object, phasing through it. I held
each student, faculty member, and staff in my caring arms and brought
them to move with me. I spoke, and I made them speak. I moved, and I
made them move. I listened, and they had no choice but to listen. I
first heard their cries — not knowing the boon I was introducing them
to. Then they began to laugh, and smile, and cry — not in sadness this
time, but in joy. The mindless babbling that came with this brought me
pure bliss. I could not contain my own laughter. With one swift motion I
was gone, on to the next school. I will enlighten them all.
