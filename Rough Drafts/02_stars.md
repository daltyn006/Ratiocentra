# Part 2: Stars

The moon and stars were near invisible in the sky, blotted out by the
illuminant lights of the city. The streets bustled with the after-work
crowd marching alongside the homeless and the dispossessed. The police
were nowhere to be seen, having been disbanded and replaced by local
militias fighting for turf with blatant disregard for the uncaring
federal government. I stepped into my tired complex about a mile from
the local park. Oftentimes I wouldn't give this a second thought, but
tonight I noticed the clouds were quite thin. I passed many strangers.
Wanting a conversation, I would say hello — some responded with
formalities, but the only replies I got were slow sighs or prompts for
cash. Many times I politely declined, clutching the loose change in my
pocket, sure it would only go toward drugs or useless extras.

Approaching the gate, I was met with a young lad. "Fifteen dollars to
enter," the boy said, "and if you don't have that, your hat would look
nice on me." His baggy hoodie and sagging jeans shifted as he
approached, smoke from his cigarette drifting into my face. "I'm sorry,
but I have no money on me, and this homburg is all that protects me from
the weather." His hand moved toward his waistband. "But I suppose I
could buy another, given the weather lately." Clearly avoiding conflict,
I reached for my brim. "While I've got you here, you'd best give me
those shoes as well." "Tell me, son — what do you want with my shoes?" I
looked down at the rugged leather dress shoes on my feet. His hand
shifted again, a familiar motion beneath his coat, and I took them off.
But my mind had been made up.

My hopes of seeing the stars rose as the lights finally dimmed entering
the park. My feet, now feeling every pebble, root, and strand of grass,
moved swiftly away from the city limits. My suit no longer kept out the
cold as the temperature fell with the mildew that wetted my bare feet.
Slowly I found a bench with a break in the foliage above. I sat down to
rest, drawing out the cigar stored in my pocket to smoke and watch the
time go by. By now, the heavens were dark, and I began to lose the spark
that brought me here. The only star visible shone quietly, enclosed by a
newly formed, menacing cloud.

Adjusting my legs to lie on the bench, I noticed a lone figure standing
in the night. "Hello," I said into the dark. "How is your night going?"
I sat up. She slowly approached, then cautiously sat beside me. Her
bright blue eyes reflected the end of my lit cigar, which lightly
illuminated the soft features of her face. She looked to be in her
thirties — around my age. The slim physique under her cheap sweater was
obvious. For a moment I glimpsed into her eyes, finding only a
reflection of myself: tall and lanky, a face worn by the tests of time,
looking much older than my age. Although cleanly shaven and self-styled,
it was clear I had not had a haircut in a while. "Decently enough," she
finally replied. "The forest has become bleak of late." The voice was
familiar to me. Its soothing femininity momentarily drowned my sorrow
about the skies. "I hadn't noticed," I remarked. "Then again, I have
been busy at my work." It was true — since the general tax increase, I
had to work more hours at my shop.

"Where do you work?" she asked. "I run a therapy practice — the hours
are long and toiling. I should have listened to my friends when they
said it was a bad time to start up." "Why is it a bad time?" she rightly
prompted. "Local ordinances have stopped providing security; banks
stopped insuring money; and the federal government keeps pushing bills
that hurt small businesses. People don't have enough extra money to
support my services — I've had to lower my price and take in almost
anyone. What do you do?" A darkness befell her eyes. "I'm..." she
paused. "An artist." Quite interested, I asked, "What do you make?"
Another hesitation. "I make music. I'm Nova." She admitted it without
pause and continued: "I rather enjoy the forest at night, although more
and more people have started living here lately." I stayed silent in awe
of who I was talking to. I began to speak, but recognizing her change of
subject, I let it go. "I can't say the same — tonight has been a quest,
though the paths are clearly marked." A pair of squirrels ran past, one
chasing the other over a bounty of food. "They look like they're having
fun," I remarked. We sat for a while.

"The birds have been quieter and less active since the change," she
said, reviving the conversation. I knew what she meant — the interstate
demolition to redirect traffic through nearby towns had visibly affected
the surrounding areas. "What happened to your shoes?" she remarked,
oblivious to the boy at the gate. "I came in from the city side — " "Do
you have a spare?" she interrupted, gesturing to my cigar. "I'm sorry,
but I only brought the one." We sat in silence as my smoke slowly turned
to ash, falling gradually to the ground in batches. She soon got up and
left, bored of my quietness.

The stars became partially visible, blurred in the night as my head
began to feel light. Finishing my time at the bench, I rose and took to
the path, feet numb on the ground. I wandered aimlessly toward an exit,
glancing at the skies once more — they looked empty. My head cleared
with them, back to sobriety. I found a paved path returning to the city,
preferable to the dirt and gravel my bare feet were toiling over. I
looked anxiously around, trying to find the hope I had found in the
stars. The shadows reminded me of my situation. Then I noticed an
anomaly.

A deep marble star embedded in the ground shone against the moonlight.
With a final hope, I strode over and looked down — there were immensely
more stars underfoot. I tried reading the engravings, but most were
illegible or lost to time. A bundle of marigolds lay on one stone; this
one was legible. Here lies Hope Grace. The stars that were above had
fallen.

Remembering my morning shift, I skimmed across the graves, my watch
showing midnight. With the path now returning to the city, and the boy
gone, the only interruptions were those asking for change — to whom I
replied with only a long, slow sigh. With one last glance at the sky,
even the lone star was now gone. My own hopes followed. As I returned, a
slight drizzle turned to a downpour. I began to worry whether the sun
would rise tomorrow.
