# Part 6: What is it to Forgive? (To Forgive is to Die)

## Part One: Kris

The haunting souls of the people I had wronged burned tirelessly in my
mind. There was rarely a moment now when their cries would pause. Ever
since that mortar shell brought me close to death, I have had nothing
but time to think of my actions. I have asked for a Jewish man to come
and hopefully give light on my situation. It was not that I wanted to
shoot those who jumped from that burning house — my commander forced it.
If I had not complied, my officers would have enforced Befehlsnotstand,
causing me the same great pain, or even death, that I had to inflict on
others.

But now that did not matter. My wounds covered all my body. I could not
see, from the bandages covering my eyes. I could not move, because my
collapsed spine kept me in place. I could not eat, from my broken jaw. I
could not feel anything other than pain. And my mind, forced into this
mortal shell, broke me further. I could not live; I could not die. There
were no good options before me. I hoped that my remorse would allow me
to ascend. I was not a faithful man, having been raised in no religion —
in fact, the only time I had ever heard of religion was that it was
unacceptable. Now, I am not sure what to think.

As the moments came and went, I hoped that someone would understand. I
hoped I could find peace at last, as so many others have. So the nurse
agreed to my wish and brought in the Jewish man. The only way I knew he
was in the room was because my nurse informed me. I had to explain my
actions and beg his forgiveness. I had no knowledge of this man — no
information about who he was. But still I asked: "I have acted in great
sin. Committed crimes against your people and the like. In the brief
moments you accompany me, I can already feel the hatred in your heart."
I continued for a while, explaining what I had done and my absolute
regret. "I hope that one day you may forgive our sins as a country. I
beg of you — at least give me the courtesy of forgiving mine." I could
hear his soft breathing stuttering as though he were about to cry. But
not for me — I had already understood this. I heard a single footstep
receding, then another. He staggered away and went back to the door. He
said no words. And I slowly withered away.

Upon my death I did ascend. The heavenly white glow told me to enter,
and I did — the pearly gates before me were closed. But instead I
noticed a blinding light once more. It spoke: "Were you good in my
image?" "Who are you?" I asked, confused. "I am God. I know all that is
and has been. It is not always true that I speak to those without me,
but this gives closure to the dead who speak with me." I had never been
a believer until now. I had rarely heard of the gospel, but it had been
exposed to me. "Please save me the conversation. I would like to know
where I will spend eternity and why."

"I would like you to answer three questions first," God spoke. "First:
have you sinned? Second: have you repented? Third: do you believe you
should be allowed in heaven? Remember that I know all." To this, I
pondered for quite some time and responded. "Yes, I have sinned — but I
have repented for the major sins. It is impossible to notice and respond
to every sin, so total repentance is impossible. And lastly, I do not
know if I should go to heaven. It is not my decision. I see no harm in
allowing me inside; I follow the set rules without question. I have
faith in my superiors. I am only human. I am flawed. But I know my
beliefs."

"Such are the ways of man," God responded. "Consider these questions:
Were you doing things for the good of humanity? Were you causing great
evil for your purpose? Did you act in resolve for your repentance? You
are going to heaven — but your guilt will bring you hell." The light of
God engulfed me, and I was in heaven. But so were the souls that haunted
me. Over the centuries I realized God was right — guilt bore into my
veins, and I was truly in hell.

## Part Two: Dostoevsky

The dark Soviet nights chilled my skin and bit my fingers and toes. I
often walked home on brisk nights after long hours at the shoe factory —
not a difficult job; I simply applied glue to the bottom of each sole.
Ukraine often lacked the same luxuries as Russia. The soup kitchens had
long, snaking lines, which I soon approached. A fellow comrade stood
there, Anna Pavlovna Scherer, waiting for her rations. "What a wonderful
night it is," I spoke as snowflakes slowly fell to the ground. "Да, я
надеялся на лучшую еду." "What is it that we are eating, then?" "не бы
очень хотелось тебя съесть." Her straightforward approach surprised me;
I was not native to Ukraine, having moved here after the Second World
War. I supposed the women here carried themselves differently.

That night was warmer than all the prior nights. Eating the crisp dry
bread and watery soup with Anna was pleasant. The time after was even
nicer — pleasant enough for me to fall asleep and continue a dream that
had begun. But in the morning, it was over. She was gone. I had gotten
my hopes up over nothing. The front door was still cracked open, letting
the winter air inside my home. I grabbed my wallet and felt a distinct
lack of rubles. But this could not matter — I was late for work. Upon
arrival, I was met by some officers. "Hello — do you need my papers? I
need to get to work." They responded: "Вы видели эту женщину?" "Yes, I
was just with her last night." I began to worry — was she lost? "Нам
необходимо забрать вас на допрос." I could feel my eyes bulging
slightly. My breath quivered, and sweat began to roll down my face. "Of
course, officers."

The prison was also being used as the police office — it saved the state
money. I tried my hardest not to panic. It was not the case that those
who saw the inside of a prison made it back out. I knew this; my mother
and father were sent here for eating unallocated rations. I, now in my
forties, had been lucky enough to have no accusations against me. "Вас
вызвали сюда, потому что Анна была американской шпионкой. Что вам об
этом известно?" the officer began as I sat in the icy metal chair. The
room was of stone; barbed wire lined the open roof. A single door to
enter and exit was barricaded with an iron rod upon my entry. A tinted
window reflected the officer and me — there had to be more soldiers
behind it.

"I had no knowledge that she was the enemy," I said, choosing my words
very carefully. One slip and I would not live peacefully. "I would never
meet with the enemy. I denounce her." This would have to do — any other
words spoken in her defense or in surprise at her being an American spy
would cause only more suspicion. There was a pause. I could feel the
eyes watching me. The officers had made up their minds, and it was not
in my favor. In a Hail-Mary of desperation, I continued: "Пролетарии
всех стран, соединяйтесь!" — I said it stupidly, compulsively.

This seemed to satisfy them for a moment. The officer left. My body
cooled in the winter air — the stress of prior moments had brought my
body to combust, noticed by the officers. They returned and began to
lead me outside. My hopes rose with each step. Then I saw Anna, tied to
a concrete pole next to a wall. And I saw a soldier carrying a gun.
"Государство приказывает вам устранить этого предателя." The warmth
escaped from my body. "устранить ее или быть устраненным вместе с ней."
I was handed a TT-33 Tokarev and a single bullet. Anna begged me. She
claimed, "Я не шпион, я хотел увидеть свою семью в Польше." But it was
not her words that broke me — I had never murdered anyone before. I had
always been a shoemaker. It was how I was raised and how I wanted to
stay.

I had no choice but to load the bullet into the cartridge; I wanted to
live. The cold winter day made me regret touching the metal gun — it
seemed to tear through my gloves and ravish my soul. I slowly raised the
gun at Anna and reflected on our night together. Could I live with
myself after shooting her? I was not sure. I knew that if I did not make
a choice soon, I would either be imprisoned or released. I made up my
mind. I barely had time to feel the cold barrel against my own chin
before the trigger clicked. But no bullet came. "стрелять в него," the
commanding officer said. And I felt a bullet enter my skull.

I rapidly ascended into light. Great closed gates arched overhead. I
noticed a blinding light. It spoke to me: "Were you good in my image?"
"Who are you?" I asked sternly. "I am God. I know all that is and has
been. It is not always true that I speak to those without me, but this
gives closure to the dead who speak with me." I had never been a
believer. I had rarely been exposed to anything beyond superstition.
"Why are the gates closed to me? I have done nothing wrong and died for
a good cause."

"I would like you to answer three questions first," God spoke. "First:
have you sinned? Second: have you repented? Third: do you believe you
should be allowed in heaven? Remember that I know all." I pondered for
quite some time and responded. "Yes, I have sinned — I have committed
adultery and allowed hatred into my heart. It was all in good faith,
though. I do believe I should be allowed in heaven. I know it is not my
decision. I see no harm in allowing me inside; I follow only what is
right, without question. I have faith in humanity. I am only human, I am
flawed — but I have always acted in good will."

"Such are the ways of man," God responded. "I want you to reflect on
these questions: Why did you make love with a woman outside of wedlock?
What made you praise another purpose over me? What drove you to attempt
suicide? For the many sins you have committed in false beliefs, you are
to be sent to Hell." The light of God engulfed me, and I was in Hell.

## Part Three: Lu Feng-Ming

Beijing was explicitly different from the countryside. It took six years
for me to observe the differences. I also listened to many matters
termed of national importance, none of which impacted me, of course.
These politics only blackened my already hopeless mood and brought
hatred for the people around me. But there was one minor incident — a
tiny thing that began to drag me out of my bad temper. The memory of
which remains with me today. The marketplace was bustling with
rise-and-shine energy; our government had come upon great wealth through
consuming our neighbors. It being Spring of 1917, there was only a
little dust in the air. I of course needed to maintain my livelihood, so
I pulled a cart of bread I had baked the evening before.

The distance between my home and the shop was great. Conflict was rare
along the way — but today was different. I was still some distance from
my destination when someone caught the handlebars of my wagon. A
vermillion gown flowed smoothly against the wrinkled skin of the
Mandarin. I continued for a moment before stopping to check on this man.
Although I had tried to swerve and avoid him, his high-political gown
had reached out in the breeze and grappled my handlebars. He was lucky I
had slowed the great weight of my wagon in the crowd, else he would have
somersaulted and cracked his head on the compressed dirt.

"You absolute imbecile, can't you walk?" he insulted me. "I'll have your
head for this!" I approached and tried to assist him in standing. "I'm
sorry, sir — I meant no harm." He smacked my hand and spat at my feet.
"Are you hurt?" "You had best get to running — I am injured in my
heart," he responded. I hastily pressed a great deal of coins into his
hand and returned to my wagon. I set off running. I looked back once to
see the old man — but he was already heading toward a police station.
The coins continued to lie flat on the ground. The day continued as
normal. There was no shortage of coins, and I soon forgot the ones that
most likely continued to lie on the dirt.

With the yellow sun setting on the red horizon, I closed my shop. I set
the remaining loaves on my wagon and headed for home. The run was quite
pleasant — but I remembered what had occurred earlier that day. As I
approached the spot, I noticed that my coins were still on the ground,
only a couple of feet off the trail into the grass. Likely the wind.
Setting down my wagon, I went to pick them up. The ditch next to the
road was menacing, only a couple of inches from the coins. Upon picking
them up, I turned to return to my cart.

A long cane ejected from the ditch and caused me to fall. The heavy
silver coins in my short gown did not help — they caused me to land
differently. I landed on my neck and could not breathe. While I gasped
for air, I saw the same old man, wearing the same gown. This time, he
laughed instead of scolded. "You greedy bastard," he lectured. "How
terribly narrow-minded — an obsession with coins. A classic case of
overweening ego. Why do you worry about these coins? You don't even know
how these came to fruition." I sat there, physically and mentally
paralyzed, incapable even of hearing his points. He went on, as my
breathing steadily worsened, fluid filling my lungs.

"Do not worry — I did not execute you without permission. In the time
that you did whatever it is you do, I sought revenge. The court approved
of this, and so it has come to be." A policeman came by: "Is everything
alright here?" he asked. "Why, of course! We met earlier, didn't we?"
the Mandarin said to the policeman. "Ah — of course, continue." The old
man stared once more into my eyes. "Well, I have made my point. Be good
in the rest of your life!" With that poorly made joke, he was off. And I
was good in the last moments of my life. There were no bad actions left
to do.

I was slowly engulfed in light. After a while, great closed gates arched
overhead. A blinding light spoke: "Were you good in my image?" "Who are
you?" I asked, expecting 'The Way.' Instead: "I am God. I know all that
is and has been. It is not always true that I speak to those without me,
but this gives closure to the dead who speak with me." I had never been
a believer. I had rarely been exposed to anything above superstition.
"Why are these gates closed? I am unfamiliar with these ways."

"I would like you to answer three questions," God spoke. "Your actions
have already determined whether you will receive the great joy of heaven
or the heavy pains of hell. First: have you committed crimes? Second:
have you engaged in self-reflection? Third: do you believe you should be
allowed in heaven? Remember that I know all." I pondered for quite some
time and responded. "Yes, I have committed a great number of crimes. I
have been greedy — I brought in coins instead of tradition. It was all
in good faith, though. I do not know if I should be allowed in heaven.
If it were my decision, I would always choose happiness over pain. I see
no harm in allowing me inside; I tried to act in goodness without
question. I am only human — I am flawed. But I have always acted in good
will."

## Part Four: Sir William

"Go, it is the dismissal," the priest spoke. "Thanks be to God," the
mass returned. "May almighty God bless you —" "Amen." And the
congregation disbanded. This would be the last mass I would attend, for
I was off to battle in the great Crusades. The castle reeked of summer
manure dried into the ground — I doubted this would fade during our
travels. I fought alongside a thousand men, leading select soldiers in
my small brigade. There were other knights in my command, and others
were superior to me. It was mostly my duty to go and fight for
Jerusalem.

My command was quite confusing — a cabinet of princes competed for our
attention — but that did not change my camaraderie with my fellow
knights. The trails were quite grueling for our men, and we saw no
battle at all. At one point we were tantalized by a Muslim relief army
besieging Antioch, but we were too distant by then. Later in our
travels, I heard that Peter Bartholomew had found the Holy Lance. I was
quite upset not to have been there with him. If only I were a
lower-ranking knight — then I might have been tossed to the frontlines.

We approached the city of Jerusalem. Our force had been greatly slowed
by the Muslims, but our cause was good in the eyes of God. The evil
Fatimid governor had poisoned our water and burned our food and shelter.
I hoped for nothing more than to send this vessel of destruction
straight to Hell. This was soon to come, as the time for battle drew
near. I, alongside other officials, agreed to break apart Genoese ships
at Jaffa to further prepare our siege.

In one final effort to appease God before our assault, we rallied the
men in a penitential procession. A few days later, it felt right to go
and save Jerusalem. Now it was time to fight. I was stationed in the
south tower, which we had moved strategically after better
reconnaissance during the procession. As I and the other knights
advanced toward the less-protected section of the wall, we bantered over
who would be first to scale it. In my heavy chainmail, I made my move —
flying toward the wall. I almost did not make the jump, but thankfully
God lifted my body and carried me across.

It was my time to fight. This side of the wall was weakly defended,
which I conquered with only modest assistance. Being first on the wall,
I would surely be written into every chronicle. My great purpose and
bravery would stand above all else. Now that the soldiers had
surrendered and been killed, it was our turn to cleanse the Holy Land
once more. Blood drained from each house and home, some of it from my
blade. Ahead, through the small slit in my helmet, I saw an alehouse to
my left.

Since the streets were now empty of adversaries and the prior homes I
had searched were abandoned, it stood to reason that any remaining
resistance would flock to this alehouse. On entry, I saw nobody. Upon
further inspection — still nobody. A menacing shadow shifted on the
wall, and I was stabbed. I was stabbed twice before I turned and swung
my sword. But I struck nothing; my adversary was too short to be hit.

I looked down. Blood spurted from my wounds. I could not survive long —
but at least I could spare one of my fellow soldiers from this sinner. I
saw a small girl, about four feet tall, wielding a rondel dagger. She
thrust once more. I cleaved at her arm. Her dagger was no match for my
sword. Her arm came clean off, and so did her head. Shortly after
ridding the world of this young devil, I sat down and waited for
assistance from my countrymen. They were just outside; all that had to
happen was for one to come through the door and call for a hospitaller.
This did not happen, even as I cried out several times.

I felt God's light engulf me. As I reopened my eyes, great closed gates
arched overhead. A blinding light spoke: "Were you good in my image?"
"God? Of course," I responded with enthusiasm. "Yes — I am God. I know
all that is and has been. It is not always true that I speak to
believers, but this gives closure to the dead who speak with me." I had
always been a believer. I had actively praised the gospel. "Why are the
gates closed to me? I have done nothing wrong and died for a good
cause."

"Patience. I would like you to answer three questions first," God spoke.
"First: have you sinned? Second: have you repented? Third: do you
believe you should be allowed in heaven? Remember that I know all." I
pondered — not on my faith; I was certain of that. "Yes, I have sinned,
but it was for your cause. It was all in good faith. I have praised you
all my life — of course I should be allowed in heaven. I know this is
not my decision. I see no harm in allowing me inside; I follow only what
is right without question. I have faith in you. I am human and I am
flawed, yes, but I have always acted in your will."

"Such are the ways of man," God responded. "I want you to think on these
questions: Was it my voice that told you to kill in my name? What
desires did you satisfy by massacring in my name? Was your purpose
completely pure?" For the many sins committed in false belief, I was to
be sent to Hell. The light of God engulfed me, and I was in Hell.

## Part Five: Fritz

I finished my painting. Human skulls piled high like a pyramid against
bleak stone brick. Candles illuminated from inside a few skulls toward
the base; a chandelier descended from the ceiling accompanied by dirty
brown water dripping down the walls. Mold crept in from the corners. I
had created a masterpiece. It reflected perfectly the suffering I had
caused — death stacked in a pyramidal pile, centered in the room. I had
omitted the leather pouches scattered behind me. Whenever I wanted to go
shopping, I simply grabbed one and went. In fact, whenever I wanted
anything at all, I could metaphorically grab it and go. I had seized
life by the lapels and stabbed it to death.

I had tortured my livestock. I bought many escorts and refused to pay
them afterward. When they began to refuse my company, I simply brought
them here anyway — opium in their drinks — and returned them in the
morning, if I wanted to. I never tried to harm anyone of actual power,
though. I lived in a grand home outside the city and far from nearby
towns. I had no family to betray me, no friends to deceive me, nobody
capable of harming me. I had sole responsibility for myself.

I climbed the stairs back into my home from the basement. My own
amazement at whoever had lived here last never ceased — they had to have
been happy. Until I came around. The previous owner had a separate
basement purely for aged wines and brandies, of which I drank liberally.
I partook often in snuffs and opiates as well, since they made me feel
good. I despised tending to livestock. I had inherited two horses, one
male and one female, three cows, one bull, twelve chickens, and four
pigs. I certainly earned enough from my fifty acres to pay someone else
to manage all this, and that is what I did for the most part.

I found I had no desire to harm those who served me — only those who did
not. This arrangement benefited both parties: they survived and were
paid; I benefited even more from their labor. With my free time I
expressed my various desires freely: drugs, alcohol, sex, violence.
Going into the inner city and robbing people at random. Fornicating with
whoever or whatever came to mind. Bearing false witness, slandering
people for amusement. One day I went into a church and spread word of
the real God — a being of shadow who wanted us all dead, and who
demanded they kill themselves.

They did not take this lightly and shunned me from their church. The red
carpet laid centerfold against beautiful stained wooden floors,
contrasting blue and yellow decorations. Various animals I had killed
were displayed as decoration as well, their bones fashioned into
miniature arrangements. I entered my library. Books lined the shelves
rising to a second story, which held even more books. I came here often
when bored. I could not remember half the stories, since I would drink
heavily while reading. I was looking now for a particular book — the
most entertaining one I owned: Aristotle's Ethics and Politics. He had a
way with words I found genuinely hilarious.

After gathering my snuff tobacco and a bucket of wine, I settled in to
read. Taking a deep whiff, I opened to a random page and began to sip,
dipping my glass into the bucket whenever it ran low. Distantly, a quiet
knock came from my front door. I turned to confirm — sure enough, it was
not a hallucination this time. I would have ignored it, but I felt oddly
sociable. Still, the desire to antagonize whoever it was proved too
great. I shouted from the window, "On my way!" — and sat back down to
read.

They lasted about ten minutes before knocking again — this time hard
enough that I feared for the door. With genuine concern, I rushed to
open it, inspected it thoroughly, and finding it intact, declared: "You
had better pay up — this door was expensive, you know." They were not
amused. "Whoever was knocking owes me one hundred Livre." "You have
continuously refused to pay your taxes, Hans Holbein. You owe ten
thousand Livre." I was not amused either. "Come in," I offered — half
invitation, half threat. "We are not here to talk. We are here to
repossess. You will come with us." "One moment," I said, and closed the
door in their faces.

I retrieved my trusty rapier from its stand on the stairwell — freshly
sharpened and polished. A shame I would have to use it so soon after its
care. But there was no other way out of this. On reopening the door,
weapon concealed behind it, I was met with something I had not seen
before: a gilded brown stick, curved into a J, resting in both their
hands with thumbs at the notch. What could this tiny thing do to me?
"Well — it was a pleasure talking with you! Come inside; it's hot out
there. Care for a drink?" "Exit the premises now or we will harm you."
"With what? Those small things?" I rapidly assessed my surroundings —
nobody else was present. I made my move and slashed at the primary
enforcer. But in a moment a sharp pain in my shoulder brought me to the
ground. A second report finished the job, and I felt my head depart from
my body.

Upon my death I ascended. The heavenly white glow told me to enter, and
I did — the pearly gates before me were closed. But instead, a blinding
light. It spoke: "Were you good in my image?" "No — I thought you were
dead," I replied. Stating as if from a script, it said: "I am God. I
know all that is and has been. It is not always true that I speak to
those without me, but this gives closure to the dead who speak with me."
I had never been a believer — and even now I was not certain whether
this was my mind or reality. I had broken out laughing whenever the
gospel was presented to me. "Please save me the conversation. I would
like to know where I will spend eternity, and why."

"You are going to answer three questions first," God spoke. "First: have
you sinned? Second: have you repented? Third: do you believe you should
be allowed in heaven? Remember that I know all." I gave this little
thought. "I have most definitely sinned — if I were merely a man. But
you are not real, I am God, and you are an impostor. Therefore I need
not repent. I believe I should go to heaven. It is my decision. I see no
harm in letting God into heaven; I follow my own rules without question.
I have faith in myself. I am God; I cannot be flawed."

"Such are the ways of man," God responded. "I want you to consider these
questions: Were you doing things for the good of humanity? Were you
causing great evil in your purpose? And did you act in resolve for your
repentance? You are created in my image — but you are not me." The light
of God engulfed me, and I was in Hell.
