# Part 5: The Universal Clock

There was no worry about jet lag, since everyone had the same clock. The
flight was generally painless. Before takeoff was the most difficult
part, since the Transportation Security Agency had become a cornerstone
of flying. Wei had tried to travel with his earbuds. The way the wires
lay in his bag made an officer mistake them for contraband, redirecting
them into a blast-proof industrial grinder — its metal jaws grinding his
belongings. His picture appeared large on the row of televisions showing
digital identifications, a newly added tally mark joining three existing
ones. But this was not much of a loss. There was a price to pay for
living under one world order. Wei stopped reflecting on this — he had a
great time ahead of him in Northern America.

The flashing lights of the city were different from those of his old
home. The airport was comparatively near empty, and things seemed a
great distance apart. Very few people had the luxury to travel from
their native continent due to the great restrictions placed on travel,
but his boss had arranged the itinerary. After a few minutes of
standing, he was met with a dark black limousine. Assuming it was his,
he entered. The American driver spoke: "Where to?" Wei, now
transparently at ease, chatted about many things, ultimately landing on
"The White House." His first of many days was spent driving there. This
was the most tedious of the time, as at each stop he got out for some
trivial reason. The two ate solemnly at whichever chain restaurant
resembled those in Asia — which were, surprisingly, many. Wei noticed
very few differences apart from the landscape. The buildings were still
square, the food still bland, and the police even more prominent.

Reminiscent of old distractions, Wei wanted his music back. While the
blank sounds of bumps on the road hummed harmoniously with the squeaky
movements of the wheel, Wei desired to sing. The window between cabins
was thankfully closed, so he did not disturb the driver. He sang to
himself in an operatic tone:

The long highway traffic intensified as they reached the District of
Columbia. Curious about the cars, Wei was again distracted. This
continued until he saw something that brought despair to his heart: The
White House. Looking around, the city appeared fine. And yet its gates
were badly bent and bowed back into the ground in grand arches. The
windows were broken and boarded up. No Trespassing banners flew in place
of flags; the gardens were devoid of flowers. The walls were
dilapidated, and scorch marks had found their way up each corner,
blackening the once-white paint.

The few coherent thoughts Wei could muster were jumbled and garbled by
despair. The photos he had seen as a child showed a grandiose home —
mammoth arches of Romanesque design descending on a stunning garden
containing all pleasant colors, a glorious fountain at its center. This
memory was replaced by what he now saw. There was a price to pay for
living under one world order. He checked his watch; it was noon. He
could not cry. His mind had gone numb. The world spun, and he sang:

The world became bleak. He reminded himself that suicide is the coward's
way out.

The driver could find no place to park. Food trucks and police filled
nearly every stop, with the occasional long-abandoned bus occupying the
spaces between. Wei, distraught, simply waited for the limousine to stop
in traffic. When the driver was slowed, he bolted from his seat and ran
out to the large sidewalk covering what was known as the National Mall.
The museums that once loomed over the central monuments were now new
monuments to the founders of salvation — each grander than the last,
leaving no room for interpretation. Wei followed the crowd past a
menacing spire reminiscent of the tower of Barad-dûr, cameras shooting
out navy-blue light against the bright blue skies. He continued over the
hill toward what he believed was the Lincoln Memorial. Cresting the
rise, he noticed there was no clear path. The crowd grew dense.

Wei noticed a speaker, accompanied by a sign language translator, though
both were nearly invisible behind the thick, reeking fog rising from the
crowd. He decided this was sufficient for now. "Welcome all to our
annual praise of our leader, Democracy," the crowd chanted in return:
"Hail Democracy." "My fellow citizens of every gender and identity, I
ask you to look around you today. In this very moment we are bound
together — not in our lives or purpose, but in our love. Our love for
each other and care for humanity. We seek one purpose: to find
understanding and comfort in affirming those in need, and to educate the
masses." "One love, one peace, one nation," the mass responded. "We will
start today with something simple, yet profound — an act of faith in our
purpose: opening your heart and soul to those next to you. Please turn
and love the people around you." The congregation began to embrace one
another. The nation did this. And Wei gave in. After some time, they
were finished, and the speaker continued. "I want you to remember this
as you leave today. Your love is meant to guide you as a light. Don't
keep it to yourself. Go forth and be what your nation desperately
needs."

Upon the wake of this, Wei felt relaxed enough to return to the White
House. The No Trespassing banners waved at him. Ignoring these, he went
on. Inside was no better than outside — ruined through years of dust and
ash, needles filling the corners of each room. He was met with an old
stench seeping from the rugs. Taking the tour, he noticed many sleeping
bags covered in a mist of blood, left behind long enough for their
colors to start to degrade. A loud thud came from the floor above, and
footsteps rapidly descended.

A scraggly man, wearing clothes Wei had not seen before, now stood —
shocked like a deer before him. "Why are you trying to hurt me?" he
accused. "What? I am here because I want to understand history." "The
only history left here is one that did not live free. Now leave here,
and never return." "What happened?" "So many people, buried underneath
the ground. When the nation becomes one, all death is from the nation.
There will be more death — humanity cannot rule on its own." The sound
of breaking glass rang in their ears. "They're here." With that, the man
was gone, scurrying back up the stairs. Loud thundering footsteps grew
closer; people casually dressed in black, wearing gas masks, entered the
room. They stood at each doorway blocking the path, removed their
backpacks, and revealed canisters of red gas which escaped into the
room. Wei was unconscious.

Waking up, he found himself in a compound — fluorescent lights flying
into his eyes while black shadows in the corners filled with masked
strangers. Two approached. "Five counts of misbehavior were found on
your digital identification. All of which went against what you signed
up to by living on this planet. First: you were caught singing in public
and causing a public disturbance. Second: you failed to report to your
workstation on your scheduled time. Third: you were recorded using
religious statements without considering all religions. Fourth: you
attempted to bomb an airplane. Fifth: you refused to stay with your
assigned guardian while visiting a new place, and trespassed on national
property. Do you have a defense?" The masked figure stated, seemingly
rhetorically.

Wei, still confused and nauseous from the gas, yelled: "I have not
harmed anyone! Most of these are not even true!" A rag was stuck in his
mouth. "Now, hearing these charges and his defense, there will be a
vote. Those who vote innocent, keep your hands down. Those who vote
guilty, raise your hands." Everyone in the room except Wei raised their
hands. "Now the state-appointed judge will decide your sentence. Any
complaints until then can be written and placed in this box." Around
five seconds later, the group bound him. He missed his chance. The
league disbanded from the room, and all Wei had left was a clock and a
camera. The clock spun wildly as time passed — it had to have spun at
least four times before he stopped counting.

The judge finally entered his chambers, bringing with him a glass of
water — the first thing Wei had consumed in his stay. The judge decreed:
"You have been found guilty by your peers and have no defense. For your
sentence, you will be enlightened, then dissolved." And with that he was
gone, on to the next room. Wei's eyes returned to the clock, both hands
pointed toward the sky. This time he spoke to it: "You have me on five
strikes? I have way more than five! If the rules were clearer, I could
tell you which ones I have broken. I do not hail this planet — I hail
the history of the world. You speak of love! What love is this? Love for
expression? I cannot sing! Love for humanity? We cannot share basic
details! We fail to follow orders — " A rag was placed in his mouth once
more. The officer was enraged. "Shut up, you idiot! Don't you know your
words are murder?!" The judge was called back to reconsider.

"Due to your recent behavior, it has been democratically deemed that you
will be vaporized by morning — but tonight you will love." A few moments
passed before he was thrown into a new cell. This cell had a bathroom
and a bed, which Wei instantly welcomed. Once he was settled, two guards
entered. They removed their clothing until all that remained were the
masks which gave them power. Wei tried to abstain, but it was
impossible. It felt dangerously good, and for a few moments he forgot
his troubles. He noticed there was fire in the guards' eyes — they did
not care for him; they only cared for how they felt using him. As his
body went numb and he began to pass out, the guards felt satisfied and
finished what they desired. Lying paralyzed on the bed, Wei began to
dream of a better place. And he was dead.
