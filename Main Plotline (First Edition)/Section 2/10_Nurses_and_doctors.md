# Nurses and Doctors

The doctors and nurses ate separately hidden in the ground. The children born into their habitat rarely saw the sun until they had reached higher status. The children interned from as soon as they can walk, those born of nurses and doctors were made to fill their shoes- often being shuffled from facility to facility to prevent inbreeding. A little colony of ants that spread in rooms beneath the ground. Each practicing and preparing for any illnesses that might arise, enjoying their time the only way they knew. Occupying the empty white pathways and bunks beneath the surface until called upon as workers that silently supported the world above. The younger children had learned of the options they had- and in the quantity of her siblings had drowned out her desires to leave, nobody else did- so why should she? 

As far as she picked up in her youth, there were three other facilities which other children had been moved between. She lived in center one, of course any center she lived in would be center one to her. She, in her luck, was born to the center she lived in along with each child was labeled on their lower backs to ensure no foul play occurred. Each children had been moved to socialize with other departments, often rotating between the nurses and doctors that forced them to run after their brisk strides. She had met many boys and girls some that had higher skills than others, her skills always blended into the crowd of others- mediocre at worst. She very often was tasked with simple nursing duties that kept her busy, and when she was not busy she would watch the autopsies done by the elder doctors. 

What fascinated her the most was the actual process she could not trail. She often found herself sneaking along the halls when she was supposed to be in other locations in order to watch the rare occurrence of an outsider undergoing the process of their test. The children were not allowed in the rooms of the tested due to how precarious the tests were, but nobody seemed to notice her missing in the minutes she would go to peer in at the art the doctors would draw on the outsiders. Over her years her brothers and sisters found their arrangements, but she was somewhat of a drifter- always going between different jobs. In her early teens she had requested to try out for the pre-processing unit. Maybe they would allow her to show how her creative side can flourish with her unusually steady hands. 

She had never known where they came from. Being born in the ground, being raised in the ground, she had no idea as to why they were drawn upon when the others raised in their little bubble did not. The markings were all of different organs, fat ratios, and any cellular abnormalities that might happen- found in the thick books they were given year-after-year. She had become friends with a boy she jokingly named "3", since he came from the third center. Another friend of hers she named "2" was her second test subject, and of course- in finishing the triad she was named "1". she had stole a multitude of styluses, brushes, and markers that would go unnoticed and waited until they had some free time before attempting to practice for the upfront position. 3 had gone from shorter than her to much taller in the short while they went without free time together. Although nights were a possibility, neither of them found the energy to finish the project. They had attempted it once before, but he had fell asleep soon after she began to mark him, and a little while later she decided to sleep as well, erasing all her progress before passing out at his side. 

She had learned very early on that if you moved at the exact same pace as everyone else, neither rushing like the doctors who had places to be, nor lingering like the children who were about to be dragged somewhere; you became completely invisible. It was an art form. When a nurse marched down the pristine, white-tiled pathways, she matched their stride two paces behind. When the elder doctors gathered around the glass to observe an autopsy, she stood in the shadows near the door, keeping her hands folded, her posture posture-less, her face completely empty. She looked like a shadow, like an extra piece of furniture, like someone whose name didn't matter enough to be called.

She was very good at drawing inside the lines. Whenever the staff handed out paper, or tasks, or rations, she took precisely her share, filed precisely into her place, and never asked for a millimeter more. If they told her to sit, she sat until her legs went numb; if they told her to scrub, she scrubbed until the skin on her knuckles cracked. Nobody ever yelled at her, because there was nothing to correct. She had drowned out her own shape so thoroughly that she simply reflected whatever room she was placed in. Her first real act of quiet defiance didn't look like a rebellion at all. It was just a drift.

She was supposed to be in the lower laundry sub-level, folding the stiff, stenciled jump-smocks, but instead, she took a left down the restricted service chute. She kept her chin down and her arms tucked, walking with the quiet, urgent purpose of a girl on an errand, though she had none. She crept up toward the upper tiers where the cold air from the surface trickled down through the ventilation grates, smelling faintly of old earth and damp concrete. There, behind the double-paned observation glass, she watched them work on the outsiders. She wasn't supposed to see the pre-processing. The younger children were kept strictly away from the testing suites because of how delicate, how precarious the initial evaluations were. But as she pressed her forehead against the cool, dark outer frame of the observation window, peering in, she realized no one was looking for her. The elder doctors were too busy.

Inside the sterile, blinding room, an outsider lay strapped to a mechanical tilt-frame. The doctors weren't hitting him or hurting him; they were mapping him. They held long, thick felt styluses, dragging bright, chemical inks across his bare, shivering skin. Indigo lines down the sternum. Violet loops around the lymph nodes and major arteries. Acidic amber crosshatches over the fat ratios and nerve clusters. It looked like an art form. A terrifying, meticulous canvas that translated a living, breathing human being into a flat, readable schematic. She stared at the markers. Her fingers twitched inside her pockets. That, was a job where you didn't have to be seen. You just had to trace what was already there. You just had to stay within the boundaries of the body. She formally requested a trial for the pre-processing unit the month she turned fourteen.

Her request was logged without ceremony, but because she had no distinct standing. Her technical scores were strictly average, her nursing evaluations mediocre at worst, they didn't give her an official position. They gave her a trial. They told her that if she wanted to prove her hands were steady enough to mark a live subject for the primary processing team, she would have to demonstrate it on her own time, using her own materials. So she stole. She slipped extra styluses, thick-tipped markers, and stencils into the hem of her bunk-mattress, one by one, over six months. But to practice, she needed skin.

Two was the first who made it easy, though he wasn't called Two then. He was just a boy from the secondary faculty track, a few years older, whose body was already permanently marked with the dark, heavy tattoos that designated him as a future surgical apprentice. He was sharp-edged, tall, and carried the quiet, arrogant fatigue of someone who spent eighteen hours a day studying. When she asked him to let her draw on him, she expected him to say no. Instead, he looked at her with a strange, tired softening around his eyes.

"The bunks are cold," he had muttered, sitting down on the edge of her frame and unbuttoning his grey tunic without being asked. "If I sit still, you have to promise not to use the cold ink." He was allowed to be vulnerable with her because he was higher status, and because in the quiet, empty hours between shifts, he was lonely. While three, a loud, squirming girl from Center Three who couldn't keep her feet still for ten seconds, flopped around on the floorboards and complained about the draft, Two sat like a statue.

Two let her touch him. It was an adversarial challenge to her focus. The anatomical editions 1 through 10 lay open beneath her bunk, their pages memorized down to the last cellular pathway, but transferring those thick, complex textbook overlays onto a breathing body was entirely different. She had to draw four overlapping lines, red for arterial, cyan for venous, green for neural, yellow for lymphatic, all describing separate functions over the exact same square inch of skin. Once she passed the initial test she could practice with the rest of the colors.

"You're very quiet, One," Two whispered one evening. The room was dark, lit only by the low amber emergency strip along the floor. He was sitting backward on a metal chair while she leaned over his shoulder, tracing the curve of his scapula with a green felt nib. Her hand didn't shake. "I have to keep the line straight." "You don't ever talk about where you want to go," he said softly. He shifted his head slightly, his cheek almost brushing her wrist. She felt the sudden, localized heat of his skin, the slight pulse in his neck. "When I finish my surgical board, they're going to assign me to the upper-tier wards. The repopulation units. They need surgeons down there to oversee the viable matches. I could request an assistant. A writer. Someone to handle names and numbers."

It was as close to a declaration as anyone in the habitat ever got. It was a hand reaching out, offering to drag her out of the anonymous mass of center-children and pull her into his orbit. 1 didn't look in his face. She kept her eyes fixed on the green line, guiding the nib carefully, perfectly, between two blue tattoos on his spine. "If I tilt the marker too much, the ink bleeds," she said shyly as a non-response. Her voice was smooth, polite, completely devoid of warmth. "Please don't move your shoulder."

Two let out a quiet, disappointed breath. The tension in his back tightened, the soft vulnerability vanishing behind a wall of cold, professional distance. He didn't ask again. She didn't refuse him because she didn't like him. Deep inside the cold, quiet space where she kept her true self buried, her chest thudded whenever he entered the room. She liked the smell of him; bleach and cold soap. She liked the way he held his breath so her lines wouldn't wobble. But to accept his offer meant becoming *someone's*. It meant stepping out of the lines. It meant having a face, a preference, a desire—and desires in the habitat were dangerous things that got people moved, reclassified, or broken. It was safer to be a mimic. It was safer to be nobody.

Two days later, the trial evaluation came. It was held in a small, windowless prep-room off the main processing track. A senior doctor stood in the corner with a digital tablet, his face obscured by a paper mask, while a live test subject a pale, shivering peer brought over from another center was held in the hydraulic harness.

"Begin the primary mapping," the doctor commanded, his voice muffled. "Four-layer overlay. Abdominal quadrant to upper thoracic. You have six minutes." She stepped forward. Her hands were perfectly steady. She pulled the caps off her markers. She knew every organ, every muscle boundary, every neural pathway from the ten books hidden under her bed. She could draw them in her sleep. She pressed the felt tip to the outsider's skin and began to draw.

She drew precisely as she had practiced: neat, perfect, hairline strokes. She didn't press too hard. She stayed entirely within the stenciled parameters. She made sure the red didn't touch the cyan, that the green remained parallel, that the yellow crosshatches were spaced exactly two millimeters apart. It was a beautiful, immaculate drawing. The doctor didn't wait for the six-minute timer to run out. He stepped forward and tapped her shoulder with his stylus.

"Stop," he said. She froze, her marker hovering a millimeter above the skin. "Look at the harness," the doctor said flatly. She looked. The hydraulic frame was vibrating slightly, a high-frequency hum running through the metal struts to keep the subject's posture rigid. The outsider was trembling; not out of fear, but from the involuntary muscle spasms caused by the cold air and the restraint tension. Her lines. The near-perfect, delicate, hairline strokes she had perfected on Two's motionless body, were jagged. Because she had drawn them so thinly, so timidly, the micro-tremors of the subject's shifting skin had caused the overlapping colors to blur together into a murky, brown smear over the liver site. In a real processing scenario, an automated optical reader would have misread the grid completely.

"You're drawing like an illustrator," the doctor said, turning back to his tablet. His voice wasn't angry; it was entirely indifferent, which was infinitely worse. "You're trying to make it look neat. This isn't art. It requires heavy, deep-tissue pressure. Bold, aggressive anchors that the scanners can pick up through blood and fluid. You're too light. You're too cautious. You have no weight." He checked a box on his screen. "Fail. Return to your primary assignment unit."

She didn't argue. She didn't cry. She capped her markers, wiped her hands on her apron, bowed her head slightly, and stepped out of the room. When she returned to the bunkhouse, Two was waiting by the door. He had heard. He looked at her with a mixture of pity and an unspoken, lingering hope, as if her failure might finally force her to accept his offer, to let him carry her with him to the upper wards. "One," he said softly, stepping into her path. "It doesn't matter. I can still speak to the department head. I can still get you assigned to my desk."

One looked up at him. Her face was completely smooth. Her eyes were wide, clear, and totally empty. She tilted her head, offering him a polite, practiced, pleasant little smile. It was a masterclass in imitation. It was a human face, doing human things, with absolutely nobody behind it. "That won't be necessary," she said, her voice pitched in that sweet, harmless, high-register tone that made people stop paying attention. "I am very content in the intake department. They need someone to organize the paper files and greet the incoming transfers."

Two stared at her. He reached out, his hand hovering near her shoulder—the same shoulder she had drawn on just nights before, but he couldn't bring himself to touch her. The coldness inside her wasn't an edge; it was a void. She had erased herself so completely that there was nothing left to grab onto. He slowly lowered his hand. "Right," he whispered, his voice hollow. "Of course." She smoothed the front of her grey apron, neatly stepping around him, keeping exactly two paces of distance between them as she walked down the hall toward the front desk. She sat down behind the tall counter, adjusted her laminated badge, and waited for the next person to walk through the door.

Five months was a long time to spend listening to the soft, wet hum of the floor-polisher and the quiet rustle of paper requisitions. In Center One, time didn't pass in days or weeks; it passed in the steady rotation of the children. They grew taller, their elbows grew sharper, and eventually, their names were called. One remained where she was placed, sitting behind the tall, white laminate desk near the lower chute, smiling her pleasant, empty little smile at every doctor, nurse, and delivery handler who passed through. She had kept inside the lines for five months. She answered every query in her sweet, practiced, high-pitched register. She filed the intake forms in alphabetical order. But every time she capped her ink-pen at the end of a twelve-hour shift, her fingers remembered the warmth of Two's spine. They remembered the way his shoulder had pressed into her palm, the quiet, heavy rhythm of his breathing while she traced the red and green overlays across his bare skin.

She had made a mistake. It was a slow, heavy realization that didn't arrive with a burst of panic, but rather settled over her like a layer of cold dust. When she finally stood up from her desk, she didn't ask for a formal transfer permit. She simply walked up to the supervisor at the barrier between departments, tilted her head, and asked in her most agreeable, harmless voice if she might carry a bundle of blank intake logs up to the domestic tiers. The guard didn't even look at her face. He saw the grey apron, the neat hair, the pleasant, non-threatening posture, and waved her through.

The upper wards smelled entirely different from the subterranean corridors. There was no bleach here, no stale floor-wax. It smelled of perfumed linens, warm wood, steam, and food—thick, rich food that never made its way down to the center-children. The hallway was lined with broad, oak-paneled doors, each bearing a small brass room number and a small, glass-encased roster card. She passed open doorways where men and women sat together at small tables, talking softly, holding hands over drink, or tending to small, woven bassinets. It wasn't the cold, mechanical handling she had witnessed during the outsider tests. It was systematic, yes, but horrifyingly gentle. An entire wing dedicated to the bonding of two individuals, and continuous, protected repopulation. A long-term commitment where one man and one woman were assigned to build a lineage over and over, isolated from the chaos of the world above.

She knew Two better than anyone in this place could know anyone. She didn't need to check the roster cards. She walked straight to the suite at the end of the hall- the largest one, designated for senior medical administrators. She pushed the door open without knocking. The inner suite was warm, lit by soft, yellow-tinted lamps rather than the flickering fluorescent tubes of Center One. Two was there, his skin flushed and slick with sweat. He stood near a polished desk, holding a thick leather clipboard, filling out the morning diagnostic logs for the rest of the unit- tracking the health metrics, cycle dates, and nutritional requirements of the other assigned pairs before stepping out to process the health of everyone else in the sector. He looked healthy. His skin had lost the pale, bleached look of the lower bunks, and his shoulders were relaxed. The colorful, heavy tattoos on his body marked him clearly as senior faculty, highlighting the muscle and tone of a man in his prime.

Beside him, resting on the edge of a velvet-upholstered bench, was a young woman. Her skin glowing in the low light, her chest still heaving in the quiet, lingering aftermath of their intimacy. She held the posture of a trained medical clerk—the exact role Two had offered 1 five months ago. She leaned against his hip, her fingers loosely woven through his hand while she looked over his shoulder at the log sheet, her rounded, pregnant abdomen subtly curving into his side. Their love was clearly not strictly business.

Two looked up as the door swung open. For a fraction of a second, his eyes widened. He recognized her instantly, his mouth opening slightly as he took in the sight of One standing in his doorway with her bundle of blank forms. "One?" he murmured, his voice rich and deep, lacking any of the old, exhausted edge it used to carry. The woman on the bench looked up as well, offering One a soft, curious smile. "Is this a delivery from the lower desk, dear?" she asked, her tone entirely kind, devoid of any suspicion or malice. "Are you ready for rounds, sweetie?" she added to Two, tapping his thigh gently before standing up. "I'll go set the tea tray in the inner room."

Two didn't look away from One, but his hand lingered on his partner's hip as she passed him, a natural, affectionate touch that showed a deep, genuine bond. He leaned down and kissed her forehead softly. "Give us just a moment, love," he whispered to her. The woman nodded, giving One another friendly, pleasant nod before stepping through the archway into the private bedroom, shutting the door behind her. One stood frozen in the middle of the room. The air in her lungs felt thick, like cold oil.

Two set his clipboard on the desk and stepped toward her. He didn't look angry. He looked at her with a profound, aching gentleness- the same gentleness he had shown her on the bunk, but now completely devoid of the longing that had once accompanied it. He had found his place. He was an administrator, a surgeon, a father, a partner. He was happy.

"You shouldn't be up here, One," he said softly, stopping a few feet away from her. "The lower staff aren't supposed to cross into the domestic sector without a supervisor. It might be too much." One didn't drop her bundle of papers. She didn't let her shoulders slump. Instead, she fixed her face into that perfect, smooth, agreeable mask she had practiced for months. Her mouth curved upward into a polite, pleasant, front-desk smile. "I am inquiring about the administrative assistant position you offered," she said. Her voice was light, high, and strictly formal, as if she were reading a requisition form out loud. "I have adequately put time in the lower chambers. I am available for reassignment to your suite. I am capable of managing your logging sheets, your diagnostic charts, and your domestic requirements. I am prepared to fulfill all duties assigned to this unit."

She offered herself to him completely: her body, her hands, her presence, worded in the most sterile, emotionless way she could manage, offering to replace the woman in the other room if he would only take her back. Two stared at her. A deep, sorrowful crease formed between his brows. He took a slow step closer and reached out. He didn't grab her, but he gently placed his warm, solid hands over her knuckles, right on top of the paper bundle she was clutching against her chest. "Oh, One," he whispered, his voice trembling with a quiet, compassionate pity that cut deeper than any blade. "No. I can't do that."

One's smile didn't fade. Her lips remained stretched in that bright, pleasant arc. "My hands are steadier now," she offered, her tone perfectly even, polite, and hollow. "I can draw the lines deeper. I won't let them bleed." "It's not about the lines," Two said softly, shaking his head. He looked into her wide, unblinking eyes with pure, unadulterated kindness. "It's wrong. I am assigned here now. I love my partner. I love the life we are building here. You turned the offer down, One. You chose to stay behind." He gently squeezed her hands, his touch warm, firm, and entirely finalized. There was no hatred in him, no desire to hurt her, only the absolute, immovable boundary of a man who had moved on and found peace.

"You aren't meant for the domestic suites, One," he continued, his tone soothing, like a doctor explaining a diagnosis to a child. "You don't want this. But you are very good at what you know. You've always been polite, you are organized, and you keep the facility running." He let go of her hands and stepped back, gesturing toward the door. "I can speak to the chief administrator for you," he offered gently. "I can ensure you get a permanent appointment at the main intake desk. A real position. A desk of your own, where no one will ever push you to do the drawings again. You'll be safe there."

She stared at him. Her mind spun, retreating instantly, frantically, into the safety of the mask. It was easier to hide inside everyone than to exist as an individual who had just been rejected. It was safer to be a mimic, safer to be nobody, safer to be the front-desk lady who felt nothing at all. Her smile stretched just a fraction wider, perfectly symmetrical, bright, and completely dead. "Thank you, Doctor," she said. Her voice was sweet, musical, and utterly void of life. "A permanent desk assignment would be very satisfactory."

As she spoke, her eyes more than water, they flooded from the years she held back. Flooded from the years she decentralized her emotions and hid behind humanity leaving nothing but everything behind. Though her smile did not fade the stream that rode down her face, cutting a clear line through the pale dust on her skin. They rolled down her face in silence, heavy and hot, dripping off the point of her chin onto her grey apron. Her smile never wavered. Her eyes remained wide, bright, and pleasantly fixed on his face, her chest rising and falling in quick yet perfectly controlled breaths while the tears continued to flow, completely unbidden, against the absolute rigidity of her fake joy.

The Doctor looked at her weeping, smiling face, and for a moment, a look of profound horror flickered across his eyes. Not horror at her, but horror at what she had done to herself, at the empty, smiling shell she had built to survive. "Go back down, One," he whispered softly, stepping back toward his desk, unable to bear the sight of her smiling tears any longer. "Go back to your desk." "Yes, Doctor," One replied sweetly.

She turned on her heel with crisp, practiced precision, holding her bundle of blank forms neatly against her ribs, and walked back down the warm, mahogany-paneled hallway, her smile fixed firmly in place, crying silently all the way down to the ground. The tears didn't stop when she stepped out of The Doctor's suite; they simply refocused, running down her cheeks in steady, silent rivers. Her face remained set in that bright, fixed smile, a rigid- cheerful mask floating over an absolute void. She didn't rush back down the chute. Instead, her legs carried her through the sprawling heart of the sector: the birthing chambers.

It was a vast, open subterranean cavern divided into soft-lit alcoves and carpeted halls, completely devoid of shame. In a world where every inhabitant had passed their sentience tests, nothing was taboo or held illicit weight; it was merely the most natural state for a population dedicated to a single, shared purpose. Hundreds, perhaps thousands, of men and women moved through the space. Some were cradling their most recent newborns, others walking hand-in-hand while heavy with child, others resting on low, plush platforms in quiet conversation.

There was a profound, undeniable warmth in the cavern. These people were not captives; the heavy doors at the edge of the upper tier stood unlocked, and anyone who wished to walk out into the surface world was entirely free to leave. Yet nobody did. They stayed because they were genuinely, deeply content. They had found their purpose in the repopulation effort, taking pride in the attempt at growing numbers, supported by a community that cared for every mother and every child without malice or greed. It was a paradise of quiet contentment. And One continued through it like a ghost.

As she moved along the central walkway, a sudden commotion broke out in one of the delivery alcoves to her left. A young woman, heavy in the final stages of labor, let out a sharp, breathless cry as her contraction peaked. "Call the floor surgeon!" someone yelled, though there was no panic in the voice, only cheerful efficiency. "She's crowning!" From down the corridor, a familiar figure came striding past One at a quick jog. It was The Doctor. Because he had lingered in his quarters to speak with One, he was running late for his on-call shift. He didn't even notice her standing there by the archway; his focus was entirely on the mother in labor.

"I'm here, I've got you," The Doctor said smoothly, his voice full of steady, practiced reassurance as he dropped to his knees at the end of the delivery couch. His bare hands were gentle and precise as he positioned them, guiding the mother through a deep, controlled breath. Beside him, his partner stepped up with a tray of sterile cloths, offering him a warm, trusting smile as they worked in seamless unison. Within moments, the room erupted into soft cheers and congratulations as the sharp, healthy cry of a newborn rang out through the hall. The Doctor held the infant up, his face glowing with pure, unadulterated pride and joy as he placed the baby onto the mother's bare chest.

One watched it all. Her eyes remained wide, bright, and completely dry of emotion, even as the stream of tears continued to spill over her eyelids, tracking down her jaws and dripping off her chin onto the cold floor. She was the only one in the entire complex who had failed, the only one who remained, standing outside the warmth, looking in through a pane of glass she could never break. She turned away and walked back down into the dark. She descended the chute, leaving the warm wood and rich scents of the domestic sector behind, until the air turned cold and smelled of stale floor-wax once again. She passed through Sector One, unheeded, unremarked upon, a quiet shadow sliding through the empty white corridors.

When she reached her main intake desk, she didn't sit in her chair. She walked straight to the front of the high laminate counter, stopped exactly where she stood, and continued to stand until let her knees give out. She didn't curl up. She didn't pull her legs to her chest. She simply lay flat on her back on the bare, cold floor, her arms straight at her sides, staring up at the flickering fluorescent lights. The rivers of tears pooled in the corners of her eyes and ran sideways across her temples, soaking into her hair, but the pleasant, agreeable smile never left her face.

When the morning shift began, the overhead lights hummed to life. 1 stood up, smoothed her skin, adjusted her badge, and took her position behind the counter. She greeted every passing doctor with her sweet, high-pitched register, processed the intake forms with perfect precision, and when her twelve hours were complete, she stepped around to the front of the desk, lay back down on the cold floor in the exact same spot, and closed her eyes.

In the days she kept her back straight against her chair behind the main intake desk, her hands resting flat on the white laminate surface. Her face was set in its permanent, sweet smile, pleasant and completely still, while a steady, silent river of tears continued to spill in her mind. When she was not performing her duties, she would lie down at the base of her desk using it as a defender from the world around her. Years came to pass and her cycle continued. 

Across the desk, her fingers twitched. Her right hand drifted instinctively to the terminal's physical keyboard, her fingertips clacking with rapid, frantic precision across the mechanical switches. *Clack-clack-clack-clack-clack.* It was a meaningless, empty motion. She wasn't logging data, running diagnostics, or sending a single byte to the subterranean servers. The terminal screen in front of her remained completely dark, a dead black mirror reflecting her weeping, smiling face. The rapid rhythmic typing was merely a nervous habit, a hollow physical pattern she had adopted to look busy, to keep her hands moving within the line of duty so no supervisor would ever question why she was sitting idle.

*Clack-clack-clack.*

The silence of the intake corridor was suddenly broken by the heavy, echoing sound of multiple footsteps approaching from the elevator chute.

One's chest tightened instantly. A cold, prickling sensation ran down her spine, not out of fear, but out of a deep, suffocating aversion. She hated looking at people now. Ever since Two had rejected her, every face in the habitat felt like an intrusion, a noisy, demanding presence that threatened to break the quiet, hollow routine she had built to keep herself numb. Deep inside the dark, suppressed corners of her mind where nobody could see, she silently loathed them- all of them, with their content expressions, their endless purpose, and their effortless belonging.

It was deeply off-putting for so many people to arrive at the front desk all at once. Usually, if any facility came in or out they moved in quiet, solitary currents a single doctor carrying a clipboard, a lone nurse escorting a transfer, or an isolated pair heading to the domestic tiers. But now, a large group of eight people surged into the white-tiled foyer together, crowding her space, forcing her to acknowledge their presence. Possibly the most people held in that room of all time.

Leading the group was a man standing six feet tall. Time had clearly passed since his ordeal; the severe starvation that had once cleared out his frame was gone, the healthy weight restored to his broad shoulders and square jawline. His skin was smoother now, the ragged scabs healed into faint, pale scars. Yet despite his healthy, solid appearance, his dark brown eyes still held a sharp, intense focus as he stepped up to the desk. Walking beside him was a short, athletic woman, just under five feet tall with bright, shiny diamond eyes and clean dark-amber hair. Her posture was grounded and patient, her movements carried with an observant, gentle grace.

Behind them came two couples who moved with the quiet, contented harmony of people who had found bliss. One man was short and remarkably chubby, at five foot five with a warm double chin and bright red hair, his face radiant with a jovial, compassionate light. His muscular arm was loosely wrapped around a beautiful blonde woman of five foot six, her soft, thin, curvy build moving with a motherly, endlessly happy warmth. Beside them stood another man... a trim, athletic thin build with clear blue eyes and neat blonde hair, accompanied by a slender, athletic brunette woman with striking bronze eyes who walked with a quiet, creative confidence.

Trailing slightly behind the group was a tall, remarkably gaunt young man in his late teens. He stood six-foot-three, his hunched posture making him look like a skeletal shadow. His black hair fell over a prominent forehead, his green eyes darting erratically around the white pathways, his pale skin marred by scabbed, bruised wrists. The entire crowd pressed forward, filling her field of vision, making her skin crawl beneath her grey apron.

One didn't drop her hands from the keys, though her typing slowed to a frantic, mechanical tapping as she kept her eyes fixed firmly on the dead terminal screen, refusing to meet any of their gazes directly. The typing on her keyboard quickened its pace. Her pleasant, high-register intake voice chimed out automatically, smooth and musical, completely undisturbed by the tears soaking her collar. "Good morning," she chirped, her cheerful mask unyielding while her mind screamed for them to go away. "Welcome. How may I assist your processing today?"

The solid, healthy-looking man at the front didn't answer right away. He stared down at her, his dark eyes fixated on her face with a heavy, searching scrutiny. The short, grounded woman with the diamond eyes stepped forward first, looking at 1 with deep, patient empathy. She glanced at the man beside her, then back to One.
