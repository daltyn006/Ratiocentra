# Recreation

The cool breeze touched Grant's nape as he lain on the soft mattress- a known touch, Lindesay's touch, wrapped him as he woke- and without opening his eyes- he hugged back tightly, breathing in her warmth. But her smell was not the same, it was clean and sterile- a hint of faded ink landed on his lips. She must have recently been in the darkroom. Grant pulled himself up- or her down- to get a better hold around her, but the softness of the mattress dissipated wildly as he touched the cold floor, why were they lying on the floor? He must have fell asleep in a bad position and pinned her underneath him, but he knew she wouldn't mind if he held her a little longer. He moved up to allow a better hold, and as the light in the room did not waver- Grant's mind did, slowly wandering- slithering back into him as he awoke.

The examination.

Grant opened his eyes to not see Lindesay's smiling face returning to him, but Manada's. Her face was unnerving to see- her hair had partially grown back into a short bob, ink still lining her face, the worst part in Grant's mind, was that it was comforting. Her smile was beautiful. Her diamond eyes and lips shown pure attraction- and the blush on her cheeks had abandoned any sensibility Grant had. He stared in emotional confusion at the view before him. The woman he longed for over many years- had refused others in hope for- was holding him tightly, and shyly smiling pressed beneath him. Then, he noticed the necklace around her neck. The emotions flowed through him, bursting through the dam he had tried to set once more, but this time he allowed her to leave. He had no use for it anymore. Manada stopped him as he walked out of the center and over to the grass, not being able to find any words to say, she instead walked right in front of him and launched her arms around his right before he collapsed. She latched onto him once more, but Grant shifted just in time to protect her from the descent- instead, taking both the blow from the ground and her head against his stomach. "Why... " Grant sobbed, "Why are you still here?" She did not have any words to say. "Why are you wearing that necklace?" This time she had reason, "It felt right, it felt right to be like you... In your reflection, I found the softest version of myself." The tears stopped flowing, and although he had no idea on how long Lindesay had been gone, some resemblance of hope returned- repairing- resembling a clogged artery in his heart that stopped the bleeding. 

The roles had reversed, and she was now pinning him- not with grief but with love. An affection worthy of nobody that set him back in his place. The tears stopped flowing and his eyes dried enough to fully see her against the pasty clouds. The expression she gave him was indescribable, although both had been too coy to interact in full, something had changed within her- and the fairness she displayed overwhelmed his mind and his heart which leapt out of his chest with every beat. Manada felt his heart begin to race, and with both of their faces flushing with something that had always meant to be. His mind and heart brought him to regain his sanity, to know Lindesay would want him and her to be happy, she would have wanted that, right? Slowly, as if he had been possessed, his hands took the necklace from her head and placed it around his, and pulled her tighter.

Manada, noticed the change of mood, shook and rose to her feet- exhibiting as much as possible, and trotted forward playfully towards the woods. Passion had swelled between the two, Grant had not known the forests that she had become so accustomed to, but the longing and protection in his heart and the desperation for something whole to return to him brought him to follow closely after her. Once the two had reached deep into the wilderness away from the pavement that any rare vehicle passed would be inaudible- she had stopped running, only to be caught and warned. "Don't you see them?" Grant's eyes were too wide open, too red from the tears, too full of emotion- desperation- and now, a new emotion, fear. Fear that caused her to cling to his arm as she looked around frantically. She might have continued her flight if anything had been around them. The memories of thousands of hands grasping at her- including his, was still fresh within her mind. And that is exactly what Grant was seeing. The shadows had been looming patiently in the woods not daring to cross the roads and instead opting to occupy every space between root and limb. For some reason, she had ran right into their residence without any warning, rushing into and splitting the forms into howling figures that spread the skies and angrily swept like a hurricane above her head. 

Manada responded after a moment. "See what?" The overcast that shrouded her was steeped in her light, and the figures were gone. She had not noticed any danger. She laid down on a bed of pine bringing him with her, and salaciously imposed herself between him and his illusions. Her words were truth when she said, "You're safe". And he was. They were truth when she said, "I think you are my destiny". They were truth when she submitted in between devoted breaths- "I love you", He could not yet return the phrase, however what burned between them was of the mind and not the flesh- a line being drawn for now, but barely.  In the morning the guilt once more hit, or was it some other emotion? He needed advice, but not just yet. He did not want to admit it, but leaving her here on the soft bed of pine needles would be like murdering her. He couldn't bring himself to do anything, the purpose to serve and love that was insatiable in his mind. And now, as he stayed connected to her, holding her tightly as if the shadows that cast from the rustling leaves would stab her opening closed scars of the past, he felt- something... odd-- something... unnerving. He felt happy.

But he needed clarity, the spectrum of his emotions had been stretched thin beyond what he could have imagined. He needed to talk to his father. Mr. Grenzer had barely left the house, waiting in a powerless and anxious state for his son to return, Grant had been gone for months, with Lindesay- Anderson, or somewhere unknown in between, only being seen in transient moments between other people. When Grant came to him last, he didn't look sick; he looked emptied. His arms were pitted with needle marks face drained of all color and skin of hydration. The stimulants held his spine rigid and kept his stride unnaturally brisk, but there was no intention behind his movement. He had walked with a heavy, rhythmic momentum, swinging his arms like clockwork, pivoting around corners without ever looking down. His face was a total void. The soft, twitching micro-expressions that usually betray a person's thoughts were completely gone. His eyes were wide and glossy under the glare of the lights, the pupils dilated into dark pools that stared straight through people rather than at them. When he spoke, his voice came out flat and level, devoid of pitch or tremor, but the contents of his words betrayed the feelings in his mind. 

The dim crimson of the home rose and fell with him as he sat alone at dining room in the center of his dwelling. He found himself unable to sleep at night- maybe he was cursed, cursed to lose a wife to tragedy and a son to his unhealthy obsessions. The matchmaking service had matched him perfectly with his late wife, so why was Grant so ruined from someone he had found on his own? He felt as much as he could for his son, but deep meditation and some alcoholic medication brought him some clarity that he could cope with. His son did not understand. He did not understand the importance of losing those who he lives for. He had no comfortable lies to tell him, there was no life after death, but the life provided to them allowed those who were willing to live in perfection.

The dim crimson of the home rises and falls with him, and Mr. Grenzer has stopped fighting it. He sits alone at the long table at the center of the dwelling, naked as everyone is naked and he lets the wall breathe its slow red breath across his chest and thinks the thoughts he only thinks at this hour. Maybe he is cursed. He knows it the way you know a rock is a rock. Cursed to lose a wife to a tragedy and a son to an obsession, and there is no one left to appeal to about it, because there is nobody up there and nothing down there and the only court that ever sat is the one the forefathers built, and it has already ruled.

He does not sleep. He has decided not to call it grief, because it is not. Grief is for the unwilling. He put his wife down the way a man puts down a weight he cannot carry up a hill — he set her into the better place and he walked on up, and the walking is the whole of the teaching. The matchmaking service had matched him with her, and her with him, perfectly. Down to the pitch of her laugh, down to the way she slept with one arm flung back over her head, and he had never once had to wonder whether he had chosen wrong, because he hadn't chosen. That is the mercy of it. That is the whole mercy.

So why is his son ruined by a girl he found on his own? Found, not matched — picked up out of the world like a rock off the ground, and now dying of it. The service gives a man a woman he cannot lose wrongly. Grant went and got himself one he can only lose wrongly, and he is doing it thoroughly, and Mr. Grenzer feels everything a father can feel and then some. He has no comfortable lie to hand him. There is no life after death. He will not pretend otherwise, not even to a boy this far gone. But there is a life with death; that is the whole of it, the only thing the willingness live in perfection, and the unwilling do not, it costs a person nothing but the one thing they will not give.

He has been drinking, and he has been sitting, and the drink has brought him to a kind of clarity he can cope with. Meditation gets you to the door. Alcohol gets you through it. Then the door sounds, and the night comes in with his son. Grant does not knock. Family doors don't wait to be knocked on, and it wouldn't matter if it did. The seam of the entrance opens and the boy stands in it, for the first time Grenzer hoped he had been wearing something, but as usual- he had no clothes; no shirt to hide the ribs, no sleeves to hide the arms, nothing at all between the world and what Grant has been doing to himself. Mr. Grenzer's own body is a heavy, well-kept thing, thick in the thigh and the chest, hair gone silver across the belly. His son's body is a report.

The healthy fat that used to cradle him is gone. The ribs stand out of his chest like a ladder seen through a sheet. The yellow is the worst of it — his skin shines jaundiced even in the dark red of the room, and the whites of his eyes have gone the color of old paper. His collarbones hold the light. His hips cut forward out of him. The arms are the part Mr. Grenzer cannot look at and cannot stop looking at: the insides of both elbows corded, punctured in lines, one site angry and blooming purple-black under the skin, and there are older ones up the forearms and the backs of the hands and one that is plainly infected on the side of the wrist. Nothing is covered. There is no covered. His genitals are drawn up small against him with the weight he has lost, and his thighs no longer touch.

The face is the worst. The face Mr. Grenzer is used to is alert, quick, the face of a boy who watches everything. What comes in is absent. It stares back at him and blinks and does not take the room in. "Let's get you some food," Mr. Grenzer says. Grant sits down in the dining room and doesn't answer. He decides to make an exception of the night and have it brought to the table. He taps on a plate and the house hears it. Minutes later the drone comes in through the roof seam, A glossy little thing, folded legs, a motor so soft you hear the air and not the machine, and sets down a covered dish, a foil card of pills, and a flask of medicated draft, then folds itself back up and goes. "Thank you," Mr. Grenzer says, by habit. The house is warm. The house is always warm.

He lifts the cover with two fingers and pushes it across.

"Eat," he says. "Take it. You've not eaten."

Grant eats. He wasn't aware of how hungry he was. He eats with his fingers. He can't wait for anything, the way a starving animal doesn't wait, and Mr. Grenzer watches him do it and tries not to let his face change. The Lame is pale and damp and gives under the thumb the way nothing that was ever a plant gives; the fat tastes of something he could not put a word to if he tried all night. Grant puts away half of it before he stops, and then he stops mid-bite and puts his hand flat on the table and looks at it as if the hand belongs to someone else. "I don't know what to think," Grant says. "About eating?" "Manada has fallen for me."

The room goes quieter than the drone left it. Mr. Grenzer sits back. He has known Manada, a year past Grant in years and level with him in class from her traveling absence, which is nothing at all in a world that sorts children by proving rather than by birthdays. He has seen her in passing with the other boys and girls. She used to be tall, a hand above Grant, straight through the spine, hips on her like a house with good ground under it; no marks anywhere; a laugh you could hear across two concourses and want to hear again. He had noticed how his son looked at her a full season before Grant knew he was looking at her. He has heard her name on Grant's tongue maybe twenty times last year. He has heard Lindesay's name on it twice. It was a name he did not hold onto because there was no reason to hold onto it yet. Now, she had failed her test, and it walks around in his son's skull eating him alive. He cannot even call up her face. 

"Say the rest of it," Mr. Grenzer says.

"I'd like some advice..."

"Say the rest."

Grant looks at the plate. "because I've fallen for her too."

"I'm glad", he put the words down like an anvil on Grants heart, lets it sit there on the table between them, and then he prepared his thoughts for council. "I know Manada," he says. "I've seen her. I've seen you when you're near her, and I've seen you when you're not, and I know which one is my son. So let me say this plainly, because you came here to be talked to and I'm going to do it."

He leans forward, elbows on the wood, and his silver belly presses the edge of the table.

"When your mother died, I sat in this room eleven days. I did not eat. I did not bathe. I sat here and held on to her, in the only way there is to hold the dead, which is in the head, and I did not put her down for eleven days. And do you know what I learned? That this was the best place for her to be, and she should be glad to have lived the decades on this Earth. There is only here. The better place is not here, and we are given the option to remain. All of us have built *this*;  the roads, the water, the food, the houses that breathe, the service that never once matched a person wrong. It's all here. It's all been here. Holding on to the grief only brings ruin. So I let her go. I know she remains on this plane, which is this life, which is my hand on this table, which is the four grandchildren I have not got yet. That's what you are supposed to do."

"That isn't how it—"

"It is exactly how it is." He holds up one hand, not loud, not angry. "You're wearing a body I have to look at. Look at your arms, Grant. Look at them. You are failing a proving nobody has even scheduled you for." Grant's jaw moves. He doesn't answer. "Manada is a year past you and level with you and better than anything that's ever stood in front of you, and you are bringing her with you into your own grave. She's told you, hasn't she. She's said it plainly and you've said nothing back."

"I said—"

"You said a boy's nothing. And she went home with it and she'll come back, because she's the sort that comes back, and one day she'll stop coming back, and you'll be sitting in a room somewhere with your arms like this remembering the one time the whole world handed you something and you put your hands in your pockets." He sits back. "I want to hold what comes out of you before I stop getting to. I want the line to go on. I want you to be alive in twenty years, and I am telling you the two things are the same thing." Grant is quiet a long time. Then, without looking up: "She's still here."

"Who."

"I can't put her down." His voice comes out flat and cracked at once. "You want me to say Manada's name and mean it, and I do mean it, and I can't, because still she's here. I see her. I take drugs so I can", he stops.

"Say it."

"I've took whatever I can so I don't have to be awake, and then I began to see her, and that's the only time I'm not — that's the only good I get." He puts his forehead down onto his own fist. "It's the only good I get."

Mr. Grenzer pushes the foil card across the table with one finger. "Take two. Take the draft with it." Grant takes two. He presses the pills onto his tongue and lifts the flask and swallows, and the medicated draft goes down him grey and salt-bitter, and he wipes his mouth with the back of the infected wrist, and he does not notice doing it. He began to stare down shamefully at the remnants of his body.

Then the hand.

It comes into the light the way a hand comes around a doorframe- the fingers first, then the knuckles, then the wrist, skin over bone, the small pale burn scar across the heel of the palm from the kitchen, the second knuckle that never sat straight after she broke it on the rim of a sink, the chipped nail on the ring finger. He knows every inch of that hand. He has spent a over a year trying to hold it in his head and failing, and here it is, and it comes down- opening flat on the rim of his plate and rests there, and one finger lifts and points- at the dish, at the empty dish, at the smear of pale fat he has left behind on it and the single dark hair curled in it, and it points at him as if to say: you.

Grant is up. The chair goes back onto the floor with the flat crack of something broken, and he is up and around the table and his arms are going out and there she is, whole, flesh, standing in the red of the room with her hair down the way she wore it in the house and her chin tipped and her mouth beginning to say his name back to him. He has his hands out and he is crying already, and he grabs her, he wraps her, he pulls her into his chest and his mouth comes down on hers.

His hands close on nothing. His mouth closes on the air. She goes off him like steam off a cup- the whole of her, hair and mouth and the burn in the palm, lifting away without a sound in one slow ripple and not coming back, and he is standing in his own dining room with his arms around nothing, kissing the middle of the room, with his father at the table three paces away watching him do it.

Mr. Grenzer does not move. He cannot see anything. His son is standing bare in the middle of the floor with his ruined arms wrapped around nothing at all and his mouth working on empty space, and Mr. Grenzer's whole body has gone cold from the neck down. "Grant. *Grant.* Sit down. What're you—" Grant steps back from the nothing. His face is running. He looks at his own hands and then at the plate and then at his father, and the shame comes up over him like a tide, because they were just talking about Manada. He just stood up out of the chair where they were talking about Manada and put his tongue in the air.

"What did you give me," he says.

"Some medication. You watched me—"

"No. What was in the food." He picks the plate up off the table, both hands, and holds it up between them, and it is empty except for the grease and that one brunette strand of hair coiled in it, and he holds it out the way you hold out evidence. "What is *Lame*." Mr. Grenzer looks at his son over the rim of the plate. Nothing moves in his face. Something moves once under his jaw, and neither of them says anything about it.

"It's food," he says. "You've eaten it since you had teeth. We had that trip to the factory when you were young."

"That's not a—"

"It is the answer I have." He pushes his chair back and stands, heavy on the floor. "Put the plate down. Sleep here. Please- I'll keep the door-"

Grant is already going, past him, out through the seam of the entrance into the night — bare and yellow and hollow, and the door closes behind him, and the house breathes its slow red breath on the empty room and the dish, and Mr. Grenzer stands with his hand flat on the table and his son's chair lying on the floor. And out in the dark, Grant walks. Not toward home; home suffocates him. He walks downhill, past the lush ferns, past the concourse, past the last of civility, across the roads, over grass and root.

There are figures out. There are always figures out — the night does not belong to the lonely in this world, and nobody wears anything at any hour, so the bodies go by him in the dark like weather. He does not give them a shape, or a face, or a thought. He does not give them a color. They are furniture, and the only thing he sees is her. She is there. Every now and again she shows — not the way she came to the table, gone the instant he touched her, but steady now, further down the hill, one arm lifted, the finger out, and the finger is no longer pointing at him. It is pointing away. It is leading.

He follows it downhill for hours, and the ferns thin to scrub, and the scrub thins to nothing, and the streets give up their light one at a time behind him until there is only ground and root and the long black weight of the sky. Then he comes up on a lone building. Somewhere in there is where they make it. He is going to find the door. He is going to open it. He is going to look. Large columns tower like sledgehammers raised to pulse him no entry. But the doors are unlocked. The doors are always unlocked, and so is the smell.

The smell seeps through the cracks at the corners of the windows, and the smell bursts from the door the moment he opens it, and it is exquisite. It is the smell of savory beef stew, of pork roast, curling into the corners of his mouth and grasping at his tongue, and the mouth of him floods before his mind has crossed the threshold; my mouth is watering, my body is wetting itself for it, and that is the part I will never forgive. The whole of him pulls toward it like something on a hook.

It is the smell of Lame. The interior is hollowed into one big room, and there is nobody in it. Not one worker, not one watchman, not one shadow with a badge. It runs itself and it has always run itself, and the door was never locked because there is nothing in this world that this world believes needs protecting from itself. The machines take up everything. Machines to move. Machines to tear flesh off bone — a long comb of bright teeth over a bed of rollers, and the teeth come down and rake and come up clean, and the bone goes away pale and dry and separate. Machines to suck out anything unpure: a throat of copper that pulls metal out of what is passing and spits it into a bin, and the bin is full of screws and pins and plates and fillings and one long coil of wire, and all of it shines, all of it is somebody's. Machines to grind, to process, to cook again, to pack, to seal, to weigh, and at the end of the room a rail of packaged meat the color of a wound moves out through a slit in the wall toward the world.

Above the first line, bolted flat and stenciled big, black on white: **THE GREATEST GOOD FOR THE GREATEST NUMBER** For eleven seconds he thinks it is meat. For eleven seconds he stands in the good smell with his mouth wet and his eyes moving along the belt like a child at a window, and the belts run at a walker's pace, one body at a time, evenly spaced, the way a house at dinner passes a plate, and he reads the black shapes coming through the comb the way you read anything you have been told your whole life is simply the way things are. Then a foot comes past him on the rollers. It is a foot. It has an arch and a heel and five toes, and the toes are curled in, and the skin of it is charred black and split and shining, and then there is a calf, and a knee, and a thigh, and the fat of the thigh has gone the pale wet white of cold grease, and the machine lowers its teeth into it as he watches and lifts the meat away from the bone in one long wet ribbon.

*It's people. It's people, that's people, that's somebody's body, that's* He does not vomit. He stands with his hand pressed flat against the housing of the nearest machine, and the housing is warm, and the meat goes by, and behind the first body there is a second and behind that a third, all of them naked, all of them burnt the same, all of them with their hair gone, men and women and in one case a body whose breasts have cooked flat against the ribs, and none of them have anything over their eyes and their mouths are all open the way a person's mouth opens when they are falling.

He understands what Lame is. He understands that the tests that sort the world into the willing and the rest do not end in a verdict — they end here, in a room with no windows where a comb takes a woman apart at a walker's pace and puts her in a bag, and he understands that he has been eating this since he had teeth, that his father handed it across the table a half hour ago with two fingers, and that his whole life has been built on a floor over a room like this one.

*I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I didn't know. I didn't look, I never once known, and every time I've eaten I've said thank you, I've said thank you like it was a-* And one of the bodies coming through is smaller than the others. It comes past him between two grown ones, and it is under the size of a forearm and a half, and it is burnt to the same black, and there is nothing about it that stops him. There is nothing in me yet that could stop at that one. I have no stomach for this. Not yet. He does not look at it twice. He does not remember it. It goes through the line and it goes away in a bag like everything else, and Grant stares at it without seeing it, and the belt runs on.

Then he sees her.

She is standing at the head of the line, past the last machine, on the wet concrete in the good smell, and she is whole. She is flesh, and she is whole, her hair is down the way she wore it in the house, her skin is the skin he had his mouth on weeks ago. Her hands are cupped at her belly, held up, cradling the air in front of her the way you cradle something small enough to need a person's whole concentration. She looks at the belt. She does not look at him. She is crying. It comes off her the way water comes off a wall in a storm, and it is a grief he can stand near but cannot read; there is a second grief inside it, layered under the first like a room behind a room, and the second one is the one that has her bent, and he has no door in him that opens onto it. He thinks she is crying for what she has seen. He thinks she is crying for herself.  *Lindesay.* He walks to her. He walks past the teeth and the ribbons and the bin of everybody's screws, and he stops in front of her, and she is close enough to touch and he does not try to touch her, because he has already learned that lesson in his father's dining room. "I'm sorry," he says. His voice cracks in the middle of the word and comes out in two pieces. "I'm sorry. I didn't know, I didn't-"

And that is when the taste comes back into his mouth. A wild familiarity that shook him. Not the memory of a taste. Her. The thing he put on his tongue only hours ago, warm and pale and giving under the thumb, the fat of it, the salt of it, the softness that was not like anything that ever grew out of the ground- it comes up to his own mouth and stands in it, and it is her, it is her body in him, it is her going over his tongue and down his throat and into his blood and it is already done and it is already his and there is no undoing it because she is in him now and she will be in him until he dies. He knows.

He goes down on his knees on the wet concrete in front of her with his mouth still full of her. His throat has already thought about it; the reflex has already gathered itself, the whole animal in him rising toward the way out- and he shuts it. He does not put her down. He does not spit her out, he does not give her back, he lets her stay where she is, warm and wrong, inside the last place on earth that still has her. It is better this way. Then his brain stops. Whatever his father gave him to stop the shaking takes the rest of him over and pulls the plug on the room- the noise of the machines goes long and underwater, the light narrows to a pin, the floor tips, and the last of Grant Grenzer goes dark with her name in his mouth.

It comes back on. He does not know how long it was. The belt is still running. He is curled on his side on the concrete floor at the foot of the first machine, naked and yellow and shaking, and she is still standing over him, and the tears are still coming off her, and he cries next to her the way a thing cries when it has run out of anything better. His face down, mouth open, wet on the floor. And then, because the mind is a coward and a machine of its own, his father's voice returned to him. He lies on the floor of the works with the smell of the world's dinner in his mouth, and he thinks: 'she's not coming back. She is nowhere. There is no after for her, and there is no after for the small one either, and the only thing left in this whole building or this whole world that is not meat is the girl who told me she loves me and went home with nothing.' He gets up. He is still crying when he comes out of the doors and the cold hits him all over, and that is when the figures come.

They had been following him on his path the whole way down and they are on the road now, and they turn when they see him come out of that building bawling, and they come toward him with their hands out. They are naked and unarmed- not carrying anything. In his mind they are enormous and they have been waiting for him and they are closing. Somebody's mouth is open, and there is a shape like a word in it, and he cannot hear anything over the blood in his ears. He runs. He runs up out of the low ground with his heels striking the road, and behind him they stop and stand in the dark and do not chase, because they were only going to ask if he was all right. He will never know that. He goes up the hill past the root and the grass, past the roads, past the last of civility the other way now, past the concourse, past the lush ferns, and the sky over the houses is beginning to think about getting light, and the house that breathes red is on his left and he does not turn into it.

He goes to Manada. She has her own place; she has had it since she turned twenty-five, a grand one- like his father's. It was not until the third room that he found her, the door the clear color of nothing like all the doors. He gently hits it with the flat of his hand and it opens; nobody withheld anything- not from each other. She is asleep, naked on the covers of her warm bed. She does not react to his entrance, nor does she stir as he approaches her in the middle of the room she has made for herself. Her hair loose and her mouth slightly open. He does not have a sentence for what he saw. He does not have one for the room with no windows, or the hook, or the bag, or for the child he could not look at twice, or for what is still sitting in his mouth. He has one sentence, and it is not about any of that. Carefully, he moves onto the bed and lies down beside her, feeling the heat off her skin. Slowly and cautiously, he moved the second necklace from his neck to hers. Her eyelids flutter open, heavy with sleep, until her gaze focuses on him resting- right there beside her- in her room. He looks into her eyes, and hers on his because that is the only thing he has learned tonight that he can keep. "I love you too".
