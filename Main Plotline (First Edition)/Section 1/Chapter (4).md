## Industry

I could see the thin needles through the reflections of my tears. What was the reason of this brief respite before putting me under? Were they giving me time to prepare mentally with the newfound knowledge of the child in my womb, or the entirely dehumanized look they forced upon me. What will they do to me when I'm not conscious? The tears continued, I could feel them stick to my body as they fell to the ground, collecting in small preserved puddles at my restrained feet. Would the doctors take these tears as samples as well?

As If on cue, a nurse with a syringe entered, soon followed by the woman who had took my, possible, last words from me. A rage burned with me "You bitch, you couldn't have waited a couple seconds to say goodbye to my love!?" "I'm sorry," she said in the same inhuman tone used at the front desk, "but since you have an infant we will need to alter your procedure slightly to accommodate it. Please verbally sign this waiver to allow this child to be trained as a doctor in your untimely termination." Only contempt rose in my heart- small embers flickered to pyre as the suggestion of my only child to become a worthless repetitive cog instead of the potential to explore the arts and rise to greater purposes than this scum. "Fuck you, no." "Your child will be terminated if you fail, good luck", she responded as if she were wishing the opposing team luck before a ball game, the same uncanny happiness scolding me for wanting anything unique for them. The nurse approached with the syringe, the tears that fell at my feet were taken just like whatever dignity I might have had. The embers burned the tears from my eyes and gave me foresight into the nurses and doctors as she left. They were tattooed in three separate places. One tatoo resided over their heart, another on their back, and a third one, in the small of their back- which was particularly unique- a large quick response code was stamped there.

A slow hum of electronics coming alive caused me to pass out. When I awoke, I was restrained in a private room- watched by at least 4 cameras. My limbs ached from being spread in the unnatural position, but the pain was comforting- it let me know I am alive, and not in the test. My head was not restrained, but I was being held in the air by seemingly nothing. I had restraints but they had no physical connection to anything, they must have been propelled by magnets? Or, maybe there was no gravity? These were the things they did not cover in the preparation, The room was hollow apart from one singular control table set directly behind me, a holographic model of me mirroring my position mocked my position. The conventional sound of mechanical movement slowly grew into focus irritating the room. Wait, It wasn't a model of me, it was me- down to the mole on my right shoulder, the only difference was this "me" had hair.  The wall in front of me illuminated, "Welcome! If you are watching this, you are having the privilege of becoming a rational being... "  I know I should pay attention but I can probably multi-task. 

I continued to watch the room around me. white tiles, white drywall, white led lights, a black rectangular screen, light blue tiles- oh! those are different. What if I look down? I'm not in a good position to see below me, diagonally facing up at a slant. An abyss? No... wait, the bright whites of the room just took a minute for my eyes to adjust-- AH this is an uncomfortable position. That's where the mechanical movement is coming from, below me was a solid metal river ready to carry me at its whim to the end. Sometime between when I was trying to look down and when I was looking at myself the man on the screen stopped talking. "If I may have your attention?" a man was in the room "Manada, if I may have your attention?" I  jerked my head to the voice. In response, he made a jerky motion at the hologram, the room started to spin. holy shit, no human should rotate that fast. "The 'abyss' you were thinking about is both the entrance and exit to your home for the year. If you succeed, you will leave in those restraints. If you fail, your restraints will be released. And you will die. Understand?" "Yeah, don't fail the test- got it." I barked at him, "Get rid of that sarcasm or something might happen while your under" I'm not responding to that... "Alright, your test will begin in three minutes" The black rectangle lit up with numbers not meant for me. "Wait! Tell Gra-" The air in front of my face vibrated, and my words came bouncing back into my ears a fraction of a second out of sync. It felt like trying to talk while another version of me screamed the previous word directly into my skull. My brain panicked. My vocal cords caught in a stuttering loop my brain choked the thought right out of my throat.

So the test has begun. 

Silence. Complete silence surrounded me in the wilderness. A numbness behind my frons alluded to the lies surrounding me, a waterfall of scrap metal silently rushed into a small stream trickling in front of my face; instinctually- I bent over to perceive the scattered pile which flew by my feet like a disorganized convoy of cars. The metal was frigid to touch and sent goosebumps shuttering from my head to my toes- a vast contrast from the comfortable sun trickling down from the conifers that tickled it back to normal. The metal seemed to be the hollow body of an antique camera- glass all but forgotten- and a thin patina that grew in patches hiding the original beauty as well as the model number. *Snap* A single sound of a photograph erupted from behind- breaking the silence of the woods surrounding the metal river. What concerns me more is the rapid pace which the metal liquifies in my hand- sickly sticking to my fingers as if trying to crawl into my bloodstream as it slowly dripped down like honey off the comb back into the now molten river. Which was overflowed with water covering the area with a thick hot burst of steam which forced me to launch back away from the danger. I couldn't tell how far I had been thrusted, the hot air scorched any hair that was on my stature into oblivion and began a ringing in my ears that distantly held the same high tone. I closed my eyes.

My lower back throbbed the moment I was gently released like the tail of a Rupert's drop to the ground, a newly automatic reflex forced me to arch my spine slightly and rest my palm under the heavy swell of my stomach to take off the pressure. I could feel the broadness across my hips, constantly warm, and bearing a center of gravity that made every step a deliberate, swaying effort. I looked over to who caught me from my fall; It was Grant. His smile exceeded my own- I couldn't remember how long it had been since I had seen him. "I'm so happy, I feel light headed-" Were his only words before his body crumpled like a piece of paper to the ground. But he didn't stop at the ground. His body meshed, not into, but through the grass, like the earth below was hollow and he had found a way to align his atoms to flee from himself. As soon as he appeared he was gone. Some part of him must have known I was with child, and he did not approve. The realization came to me, erupting like a volcano in my soul, He did not have the right to leave me and my child- I wouldn't let this happen. I blinked and nothing remained. 

My mind was blank, I could remember language but putting words together strained my processes and tired me vastly.  The restraints are there. I can feel them. They press into my wrists, my ankles, like a second skin, a constant, unrelenting weight. But the rest of me? The rest of me is gone. I would look if I had eyes, I would react if I could know.
There is no thought. No memory. No words. Just the faint, rhythmic pulse of my heartbeat, like a drum in the void. I am not thinking. I am not speaking. I am not even me. The restraints are real. They are physical. They are outside me, and yet they are part of me. They are the only thing I can feel. The only thing I can be. My mind is not here. It was taken. Not in a flash, not in a scream. It was taken slowly and peacefully, like a child drowning in a lake. I don't know what it was. I don't know what it felt like. What have I lost? All I know is this: the restraints are outside me. And I am inside nothing. I could feel movement in my stomach.

Colors adjusted around me, shifting and reflecting abstracts that fully removed me from whatever reality held. I must have struggled against the restraints... yes, that must be it. The colors grew to contrast and fill into shapes. Simple at first, then it slowly came into focus imitating organic life in a poor impersonation of a vixen. At first I could not speak, after an extended period I noticed colors absorbed into my body and I tried once more, I stumbled through the dense timber, my knees buckling as my boots sank into thick, damp moss. The sudden transition from nothingness into the raw stillness of the woods hit my senses like a physical blow. Everything smelled intensely of rotting pine needles and cold, wet loam. My heart pounded against my ribs—not just from the abrupt physical shock, but from the sudden, terrifying realization that I was utterly alone. I couldn't remember my name, how I had gotten here, or what my life had been before this dark tree canopy. My past was a smooth, blank void, leaving me with nothing but the immediate, sharp instinct to survive and the words to achieve it.   

A rustle in the underbrush froze me in place. From the shadow of a fallen log, a vixen emerged. Her fur was a vivid, burning rust against the dull grey of the forest, her amber eyes locking onto mine with an unblinking focus. She moved with a sharp, limping gait, her belly distended and heavy, swaying low to the earth. She was feral, wild, and heavily pregnant. I held my breath. The vixen didn't run; she merely watched me, her sides heaving in shallow, rapid pants. In her eyes, there was no wild savagery, only a desperate, straining endurance. An overwhelming, illogical wave of protectiveness washed over me. I didn't know who I was, but I knew with absolute certainty that this mother and the life inside her could not die here. Before I could take a step toward her, a low, guttural growl vibrated through the earth beneath my feet. The trees groaned as a massive brown bear crashed through the thicket.

 Its bulk was staggering—a towering wall of muscle, matted fur, and scarred hide. But its eyes didn't hold the chaotic hunger of a wild beast. It carried the warm, soulfully measured detachment of an entity that had passed through the threshold, a creature deemed "rational" by the system, looking down upon the unsanctioned, feral life before it as nothing more than raw material. Its dark eyes swept the clearing, anchoring directly on the vulnerable, limping vixen. It took a heavy step forward, jaws opening to crush her in a meaningless assault. "Stop!" I screamed. I threw my self into tumble directly between the giant beast and the trembling vixen, shielding her with my body. I had no weapons, no physical strength to match an apex predator, but a desperate, animalistic defiance flared within me. "You won't touch her! She is carrying life!" The bear did not rage. It halted, looking down at me with an eerie, intellectual disgust. When it spoke, its voice was a low English, resonant rumble that seemed to emanate from the air itself. "Why preserve that which has no standing?" the bear asked, its tone dry and analytical. "Look at her. She is feral. Her offspring will be born into a world that has no place for the chaos of the wild. They will suffer cold, starvation, and inevitable culling. To allow those kits to be born into a life of absolute, unmitigated agony is not mercy—it is an indulgence of your own ignorance."   

"It's life!" I stammered, my mind racing as I grasped wildly for a foundation to build an argument, but finding only empty air where my knowledge should have been. "They... they deserve a chance! Every living thing wants to live. It's... it's wrong to cut it short before it starts!" "Desire is not purpose," the bear countered, taking a slow, deliberate step closer. "A plant turns toward the sun, yet it possesses no agency. You plead for continuation out of a blind, instinctual horror of death, not out of logic. What purpose does their survival serve? What utility do they offer the grand design? If they are born, their existence is merely a cycle of pain ending in the slaughterhouse or the frost. Ending them now, before consciousness takes root, is the only rational kindness." The bear circled around in predation.   "No!" I yelled, reaching back to feel the vixen’s frantic heartbeat against my leg. I did not expect a nip at my fingers for getting too close. My chest felt tight, a suffocating weight pressing down on my lungs. "It's not just instinct! It's... love! The mother feels it—I feel it! You can't just measure everything by whether it's useful! If we kill everything that suffers, then nothing matters!" My words felt small, fragile, and pitifully weak against the cold, iron clad certainty of the bear's logic. I was stuck between two predators in a battle of wits.

I couldn't articulate why a feral creature's life had value beyond its suffering. I had no philosophy, no data, no higher framework to expose—only the screaming, stubborn impulse of a mother wanting to shield a child. I was clinging to the mud while trying to argue with the sky. "You offer no proof of moral agency," the bear stated flatly. "You offer only reflex." The bear didn't attack. It simply turned away, its point made, leaving me kneeling in the damp moss beside the trembling vixen.I buried my face in the vixen's thick coat, sobbing silently, clutching her to my chest as if I could protect her from the very logic of the universe. It did not bite this time. But as I held her, the forest around us began to shift. The rich emeralds of the moss, the deep russet of the vixen's fur, and the dark canopy above began to lose their saturation, draining away into pale, monochrome greys. It was as if the signals feeding the world were being systematically cut. Tiny, brilliant points of light—like billions of electrons firing across a hyper-dense grid—began to pulse through the trees, the dirt, and my own hands. The world was resolving into billions of rapid, binary flashes, flickering out section by section, restoring my brain to its baseline state. The scent of pine dissolved into the sharp, chemical tang of ozone, and the warm weight of the vixen evaporated beneath my arms, leaving me suspended once more in the dark, silent void between states.

The static grey of the memory-wipe simulation did not remain void. Instead, it reassembled itself with terrifying, instant familiar detail. I found herself standing back in the photography darkroom, surrounded by the heavy, suffocating scent of the perfume, sweet cherry, and more recently Grant. Hanging drying lines crisscrossed the ceiling, holding black-and-white exposures of their experiences. On the table in the corner sat her heavy brass tripod.   Everything felt entirely, undeniably normal. One difference I noticed was my new vigor, which led me to sit on the bed centered in the room and wait with more patience than I should have had. I looked through the beautiful dome above to watch the stars, and the sun smeared across the canvas of space into one confused mental image that persisted in my mind. It must have been months I lain there, waiting for someone to break the spell.

The heavy velvet curtain at the entry rustled. Two figures stepped into the warm amber light of her studio. They were obscured, shifting shapes—dark, featureless silhouettes outlined in a cold, flickering luminescence. They carried no distinct faces, no recognizable eyes, only the faint, unsettling suggestion of one male, and one female form. Yet, as they moved into her space, an innate, unquestioned certainty flooded her mind: they were her parents. "Manada," a voice echoed from the lead silhouette. It didn't sound like a whisper; it sounded like an imperative arriving directly inside her brain. "You are in the final tier."   She took a step back, her hand reaching out instinctively to grip the edge of her worktable. "What are you doing here?"   "We are here to save you from our mistake," the second shadow pleaded, its form trembling like static on a dead monitor. "Look at what you are holding onto, child. You are clinging to a ghost." "I'm not clinging to a ghost," Manada argued, her voice tightening as her mind desperately constructed a defense. She gestured around the room, to the hanging prints of Grant, to the camera mounted on the brass tripod. "I have a purpose here! I capture truth. I process what people are too afraid to look at. My artistry—my work—it proves I am something more!"   "Artistry is merely an aesthetic loop," the caring voice of the lead silhouette countered flatly, stepping closer. "It is a refined reflex of the ego, not higher logic. You use your lens to record Grant because your basic evolutionary hardware demands attachment to a mate. You defend the unborn because your nervous system is trapped in a primitive loop of maternal preservation."   "It's not just a loop!" Manada cried out, her chest heaving as she fought against the cold, suffocating weight of their argument. 

"Grant needs me! He's terrified—he lives every day under the weight of this society, waiting for the floor to fall out. If I give him up, if I abandon the life inside me, then my existence means nothing! Caring for my child, keeping Grant anchored—that is my higher reason!"   The shadowy figures wavered, their presence growing heavy with an unbearable, emotional grief. They did not answer her with cold equations; they reached out with shadowy, trembling arms, begging her with desperate, maternal terror. "Please, Manada—listen to us!" the second figure wept, its voice breaking into feminine jagged, distorted frequencies. "We thought the same way! We believed our love for you was a noble, higher truth, and it cost us everything! We were erased! We were slaughtered and buried beneath the pale stone because we refused to yield our personal attachments to the machine's grand design! We thought we knew better than society and that cost us everything!" "Then you died for something real!" she yelled, tears spilling hot over her cheeks. "No! We died for nothing!" the lead shadow pressed, its voice rising in frantic, agonizing appeal. "Our death didn't stop the machine! It didn't alter the society! It only left you an orphan, defenseless and alone in a world that used you as raw material! You must show them you possess a utility beyond your instincts! You must reason through why your continued survival serves the broader whole! If you cannot give up Grant, if you cannot abandon your child in your mind, you know what will happen!" Manada stood frozen in the amber glow of her darkroom, her hands trembling violently against the worktable.   

I tried to think. I tried to construct an equation, a grand philosophical proof that would satisfy the cold, unyielding evaluation of the test. I tried to find a logical argument for why a mother's devotion or a lover's touch possessed objective final value to a society built on utility. But as I reached desperately into my mind for logic, all I found was the memory of Grant's pulse against mine. I found the memory of his rough hands at her waist, the taste of his skin, and the fierce, animalistic need to survive long enough to hold the child that had been taken from her belly. She couldn't rationalize it away. She couldn't reduce her love to a utilitarian asset. She didn't want to die, but her entire drive to exist was bound to the very biological, instinctual anchors the machine deemed "abnormal and unique to criminals ". "I can't," she sobbed, stepping back until her spine hit the now freezing bed. "I can't give them up. If I let go of him... if I let go of my baby... there is no 'me' left to survive." The two shadowy figures bowed their heads in quiet, tragic surrender. "We did all we could do." the lead silhouette whispered softly, the tone of finality speared my heart and drained me of hope. The amber light in the photography studio didn't flicker out—it cracked. The room fell away as if it were never there to pure light, leaving only the remaining velvet curtain. As much as I hoped to stay, I had to leave.

I was still suspended in that stretched-out X position, wrists and ankles locked in rigid, immobile cuffs, completely unclad and mapped in neon-glowing gridlines.   My stomach was as flat as the day I had arrived, but the timer overhead read five minutes left. Oh shit. I had given birth. I had given birth right here, locked in this metal frame, my body forced to push out a child while my mind was lost in the woods. I was utterly, violently furious. I had been robbed of my own child's birth, cheated of the single moment of warmth I was owed. I squirmed franticly against the rigid clamps, my neck straining as my eyes darted across the sterile walls, searching for any clue as to where those monsters had taken my baby.  

The heavy door hissed open. The sharp, piercing sob of a crying newborn entered the room first, echoing off the cold steel.   Following the sound was the woman from the front desk—the receptionist with the laminated badge and the high, squealy voice pitched for comforting stupid children. She hadn't aged a single day. She stepped into the center of the room, holding a tiny, squirming bundle wrapped in industrial cloth, her face wearing that same vacant, performative smile.   I strained against my wrist holds, twisting my body toward her. "Where is he?! Give him to me! Please—let me see a doctor! Bring a doctor in here!" I pleaded, my voice cracking into jagged, desperate shrieks. "There has to be a mistake! Let me retake it—no take-backsies! Please!"   "Oh, sweetie, there are no doctors for you now," the HR lady cooed in her sickly, condescending pitch, not looking at my eyes, but looking at me like a failed ledger entry. She sighed, shaking her head with patronizing pity. "You really showed such appalling, base inhumanity out there. We gave you four distinct scenarios to demonstrate higher logic, and you just kept clinging to primitive, biological filth. Driven by raw, self-serving animal instinct. You couldn't even reason through basic utility."   

She took two steps toward the open hatch in the floor beneath my suspended frame.   "Honestly, it would have been so much more rational to have had the baby closer to eighteen," she added offhandedly, tilting her head with a light, dismissive chuckle. "At least by then, if you'd managed to prove sentience, he'd have a designated utility code. But this?"   Without a flinch, without a flicker of remorse, her hands opened. She dropped my crying infant straight down into the dark opening beneath us.   "NO!" I screamed, a sound that tore through my throat until I tasted blood. The woman casually strutted over to the holographic table. With a singular unimportant gesture, the overhead clamps instantly released their grip.   My limbs, locked rigid in a hyper-extended state for an entire year of agony and childbirth, refused to bend. I plummeted thirty feet down into the dark chute, crashing violently onto the moving steel of the conveyor belt below.   The impact slammed the wind from my lungs, a horrifying, tearing pain shooting through my shoulders and pelvis. But there was no time to fracture. The belt was moving fast—running at a punishing, sprinting pace, carrying me through a vast, subterranean transit line.

 The corridor was pitch black, illuminated only every few yards by the piercing, blinding white glow of the testing rooms spanning above. Thousands of them lined the ceiling, silent and sterile, each holding some other poor soul undergoing their year-long nightmare. I was completely alone on the belt—just me and my baby. I gasped, dragging my stiff, unresponsive limbs across the moving scrap that cut and tore at my skin. My hips burned with raw agony, my muscles locked up like cold iron, making it impossible to crawl fast enough against the belt's rapid loop. A few feet ahead of me, the tiny bundle lay still. I swam through the jagged metal forward, scraping my chest along the steel until my fingers hooked into the cloth. I pulled him to my breast. He was a boy. His little body was cold and still, but as I pressed my ear to his chest, I felt a tiny, erratic flutter. He was alive, but unconscious. "I've got you," I sobbed, pulling him against my bare, inked skin. "I've got you...". Only unknown remained ahead. Above us, the high, impossible square of the exit hatch faded into the ceiling, completely unscalable. With my last hope, I threw my child as hard as I could. My stiff, delicate arms moving him inhumanly fast from the pure adrenaline running through my veins. He had to survive.

Ahead, the steel tracks simply vanished over the edge of a massive, yawning abyss—a hellish black entrance into the earth. The belt hurled no metal over the precipice. It was meant to transport something else. My child came down as quickly as he raised. Gravity had cursed his chances of life. The terror of the fall swallowed me whole. In a last attempt of protection, I scrambled desperately to reach my child- maybe there was an exit at the bottom if he survived. Gravity ripped us down into the absolute, suffocating void. The wind roared in my ears like a jet engine, tearing at my skin as we plunged into the black. I tried to hold him—I tried with every ounce of strength left in my mangled arms—but the sheer force of the terminal velocity wrenched my stiff fingers apart. He was ripped from my grip.   "NO!" I fell for ninety agonizing, endless seconds. Ninety seconds of screaming into the dark, waiting for the floor, praying for the end. 

*CRACK*. The stop was an explosive, shattering catastrophe. The impact shattered my feet, driving my femur bones straight up through my knees and hips. Both of my legs shattered instantly, snapping into jagged pieces beneath me. A sickening, wet squelch absorbed the rest of the momentum. I didn't hit concrete. I landed in a deep, rotting mountain of soft, fresh human and animal flesh—centuries of culled bodies, piled miles beneath the civilized world. I lay crumpled in the suffocating, pitch-black silence, gasping for air that smelled of smoke, ancient rot and copper. The raw, physical agony of my pulverized legs radiated through my spine, a screaming, blinding fire that made every micro-movement feel like dying. Why did I land on my legs? I wept silently into the dark, my tears mixing with the slick fluids of the corpses beneath me. Why am I still breathing? Please... let someone fall on me. Please, let the next one drop right on my head and end this. I dragged myself forward using only my elbows, my useless, shattered legs trailing behind me like dead weights over the cold, clammy skin of the dead. I crawled through the absolute, lightless void, feeling blindly in every direction, digging my fingers into raw, lifeless limbs, searching every corner of the pit. I couldn't find my son. I knew he was dead. I knew I was here, miles under the earth, broken, discarded, and dying in the dark. And as I collapsed face-down into the cold heap of the forgotten, clutching at empty air, I knew no one was ever coming to save us.
