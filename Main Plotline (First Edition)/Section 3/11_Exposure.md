# Exposure

"You? There are no relations between us." The woman at the counter responded, "You must be mistaken." She looked more confused than anything, there was no way that an outsider, a person from above, had any relation to her. Still he did not leave- so she asked, "Why are you here?". "We're here for you, I... I'm here for you." There shone the slightest glimmer of hope in his eyes, a lighthouse that tried to bring her home to shore from the void that falls on the ocean at night. "And who are you people exactly?" "Besra, I'm Grant- your brother, this is my wife Manada. Anderson and Eira, and Christoph and Giesla." "Yes, you are due for testing, would you like to begin early?" She said looking at the empty terminal- she knew this was a lie, she remembered the emotions that led this man astray. There was some admiration for the depth he allowed his emotions to reach. She wanted to leave, she wanted to go back to her idle job doing nothing. She had never gone outside. In the 11 years at the front desk, only he had the audacity to come in and try to throw himself down the chute. In the 11 years at the front desk, she had never even thought of touching the sunlight that blinded her each day.

She thought that if she tried to leave the skies would descend on her and devour her whole, any movement that was un-regulated would do that. She had thought to jump as he did. She wanted to leave, go somewhere that was away from herself. "Have you ever thought about who your mother or father are?" The man named Grant questioned her with a determination that she had never seen below. Her hatred for him only intensified, "There is no reason for questions, please exit if you are not here for testing." "Besra," She hated the name already, "Please, at least come meet your father- he'll be happy to see you." "Please leave." Her smile descended from its high peaks, the full preparations she had made to rot at the front desk faded in front of her as he pushed her around. The noticeable change in her demeanor shook the man claiming to be his brother, "Are we getting you into trouble? Please get approval, I want to get to know you- we, we would like to get to know you." 

"No," she said before thinking of what he said- her discomfort rose like a bonfire doused in gasoline, "I haven't known you- I don't want to know you, please leave." She loved her tedium. She lived in her spot, there was no home for her outside. There was nothing for her. Maybe, if he had come in years past there had been a chance, there would be some decision she would make against her better judgement to leave. Maybe, if she had been better at what she did he would never have found her. But he was here now, he was trying to move her. He was trying to give her another chance. The resemblance of who she used to be- the girl that wanted to see things she couldn't see. The girl that had hopes and dreams for a future she had burned was dead, replaced by a thing. An empty thing that did not feel, or at least was not supposed to feel. Grant walked around the counter, crossing her barrier physically and emotionally when he gently wrapped his arms around her. She hoped that someone would let her leave, she wanted to go back to the world she knew- the world she was separate from. She hoped to an entity that was not there for him to leave her alone, but he held on. He always found a way to hold on.

Then Grant walked around the counter. He crossed the physical line, stepping into her sanctuary, and wrapped his arms around her. Her body locked instantly. She had not been touched in eleven years. It was unprofessional; it was forbidden; it was a violation of every rule that kept her alive. The smell of him, of the air from above, of sweat and dust and real sunlight, flooded her lungs, sickening her. She waited for him to let go, staring blankly over his shoulder at the empty hall, praying to whatever entity governed the facility for him to release her. He didn't. He held on with a terrifying, desperate strength.

Before her mind could process the intrusion, shadow fell across them. Anderson moved in next, stepping over the threshold without permission. Then came the others, drawn in by the momentum of Grant's conviction. One by one, bodies pressed against her. Fabric rubbed against fabric; hot breaths collided in the cramped space behind the desk. They enclosed her entirely, building a cage of physical heat. To them, it was an offering—a reunion, a restoration. To her, it was simply the overwhelming, suffocating weight of strangers' skin. She was trapped at the center of them, both more hidden and more exposed than she had ever been in her life. The rigid structure holding her together shattered.

A tear slipped free, hot and silent. Then another. The sound that escaped her chest was not a gentle sigh of relief, but a ragged, terrifying gasp as eleven years of carefully guarded silence caved in. The noise caught in the small space, spreading like a contagion until the group around her began to weep too; closing their arms tighter, believing they were sharing in her healing. But as the room filled with their tears of joy, hers remained the only tears of absolute grief. 

The storm inside her didn't resolve; it simply ran out of air. As the group's tight grip slowly loosened, Besra felt the familiar, cold numbness flood back into her chest. The circuit had tripped. The tears stopped as suddenly as they had begun, leaving her face wet and stiff in the air conditioned draft. She looked through the group and saw the hatch that led to the conveyer.

Grant pulled back slightly, his hands resting gently on her shoulders. His face was flushed, radiant with the misspent joy of a savior. "I am begging you, Besra," he whispered, searching her vacant eyes. "You don't have to stay here anymore. Please Besra, come with us." In the past, she had tried to choose. She had tried to decide what was best for her, wanting to leave a mark, and had been corrected. She had tried to reach for The Doctor in the breeding district, believing in a shared future, only to be left behind at this desk while he ascended. Resistance was a desire. Staying was a desire. She could not give in to her desires. To survive, she must be nothing.

"Okay," she said. The word was hollow, devoid of warmth or consent. It was simply the sound of a door being pushed open by a force larger than herself. Grant beamed, delicately holding her hand. She didn't squeeze back, but she didn't pull away either. She looked down at the empty screen one last time. Eleven years of smooth glass, gray metal, and blinding morning light. She had no possessions to carry, nothing holding her in the position she resided in. Then, letting Grant pull her by the hand, She stepped out from behind the counter for the first time in four thousand days.

She tore herself from his had standing directly behind the line where the shadow ended, terrified of the infinitely luminescent sunlight that threatened to liquefy her skin. The clear glass doors that were always open now laughed at her, daring her to leave. Beyond the threshold, the world opened up into a sickening, infinite expanse. There was no ceiling. The morning sky above the parking lot was not blue, but a bruised, suffocating blood-red; a violent morning stain that stretched out forever, offering no walls, no corners, no boundaries. The vast open air didn't feel like space; it felt like being dropped into the middle of a bloody, bottomless ocean, where taking a single step meant suffocating in the sheer weight of empty air.

"Come on, Besra!" Grant called out, his voice loud and brimming with a terrifying, thoughtless excitement. He turned back and grabbed her wrist, his grip warm and solid, trying to yank her out onto the asphalt. He was moving too fast, desperate to drag her out, completely blind to the fact that stepping across that threshold was flaying her alive. "Grant, stop!" Manada's voice cut through the hot and humid air. She stepped forward, placing a firm, grounding hand on Grant's arm to pull him back. She looked at the rigid, frozen shape of the girl that had collapsed to the safe, stable ground - clawing and scrambling herself back inside, hyperventilating at the door. A room kept a person contained. A desk gave her protection, a perimeter, a fine line. But out here, beneath a red sky that stretched on without an end, she felt her fake smile and hollow skin unraveling. Without walls to hold her in, her nothingness spilled out into the atmosphere, becoming as thin, meaningless, and endless as the blood-colored air above her. Manada rushed to comfort her, "Look at her. You're rushing her. Let her go at her own pace."

Grant blinked, his face falling slightly as he loosened his grip on her wrist, but he didn't let go entirely. "I just... I want her to see. I want her to be free." Free. The word made the cold void inside her coil with a sickening, silent hatred. She stared at Grant's eager face, then past him at Manada, and then toward the idling vehicles where the rest of the group was disappearing. She didn't see people. She didn't see a brother, or a sister-in-law, or friends. She saw things. Loud, fleshy, moving things that operated with an arrogant, unearned warmth. They had found their places. They had found their matches, their purpose, their love, things she had buried.

She hated them. She hated them for dragging her out into this uncapped red hell. She hated them for demanding that she be a person when she had worked so hard to become nothing at all. "Please," her voice came out as a sweet, high-register chime, completely detached from the absolute horror clawing at her mind. Her pleasant, front-desk smile stretched across her face, bright, symmetrical, and completely dead, while her eyes remained wide and vacant to reflect the blood-red morning light. Manada could not have meant well, her touch felt like cancer on her skin. A disease that had been lost to time, a disease that spread from the warmth of her fingertips. Something that wanted her to die, something that made her write under contact- lasting only for a moment. "Don't be ridiculous. I am ready to leave." Her movements held nothing back; she was walking straight into the mouth of the thing she feared most, simply to prove it could consume her. She drove her feet forward across the threshold. The shadow split beneath her shoe, and the raw, unshielded sunlight slapped against her skin like scalding water.

Every wire in her nervous system screamed. To her mind, the floor vanished beneath her feet, replacing the asphalt with a sheer, infinite rise into a blood-red abyss. The air was too thin, too wild, too vast- a roofless ocean that threatened to strip the flesh from her bones. But instead of flinching back into the doorway, she leaned into the fall. She pressed forward, accelerating her stride until she was gliding across the open, sun-drenched lot with a swift, mechanical disregard for her own life. She walked as if she were stepping off the roof of a high-rise, waiting for the impact. Let the sky fall. Let the radiation dissolve the eyes. Let the air burn the lungs. It was a quiet, furious dare hurled at the universe- a suicidal gamble that if she walked far enough into this uncapped hell, she would finally cease to exist altogether.

Grant's face lit up with a blind, triumphant joy. "Look at her!" he laughed, turning to Manada, completely misreading the velocity of her steps as eager excitement. "I told you! She's ready!" Behind them, Manada stayed frozen, her hand still hovering in the air where she had touched her. Her dark eyes narrowed as she watched the girl's rigid, perfectly upright back retreat toward the waiting vehicles. She didn't see a sister stepping into the light of a new morning. She saw a body throwing itself into a fire, smiling all the way down.

Manada kept a close eye on her in the vehicle. Although her smiles were endless and her actions were perfectly fine there was only death in her vision. The deadpan practice she showed made her wonder if Besra was even human- or some sort of thing. Manada knew Besra was afraid, more afraid than she had ever been in her life- so why was she not acting it? The tattoos that labeled her body gave her something to interrogate her with. "What do the markings mean?" "They are formal placeholders to mark the positions, statuses, and restrictions set upon the people in the medical units." "What does yours mean?" "Mine is one that has been released to the front desk. I am not allowed in the lower units except for seeing those after testing."

Manada's fingers tightened on the edge of the seat cushion with concern. The answer had been delivered with the smooth, practiced elegance of a machine reciting a manual, yet Besra's smile had only widened- a bright, symmetrical arc of fake warmth darkened the air around her in the vehicle and made it feel ten degrees colder.  "And the lower units?" Manada pressed, her voice dropping to a low, cautious murmur so Grant wouldn't hear over the hum of the engine. "Why were you restricted from them?" "Rules are established for efficiency," Besra chimed softly, her eyes fixed on the blood-red horizon rushing past the window. "When a cog has no further use, it is relocated to a desk. It is very simple." Grant had hardly took his eyes off of Besra, his face was lit up with a blind, joyful warmth. "It's perfectly okay, Manada. Don't grill her on the facility stuff already. She's probably had a long day." He met Besra's glassy gaze and smiled. "We're going to make up for all those lost years, Besra. You don't have to worry about that anymore, you're going to have a real home now." Besra's smile didn't alter by a millimeter. In her mind, she hoped the car would crash into the sky. an endless ocean of blood and light trying to crush the glass. A real home? She could not comprehend such a thing.

The winding driveway had thrown Grant and Manada together on the bench, it was clear Besra had become the third wheel. The sky cleared into a glassy blue that allowed the night to slip in from behind the curtain. Grant had not seen his father in quite some time. His escapade to for whom he was destined was never relayed to him, and it showed in his movements towards the door. The apprehension taught on his face, casing him to hesitate at the door. "Let me get that for you sweetie." Besra said excitedly. She walked directly into the glass like it was a curtain that would give way to her form. The deep crimsons of the hallways comforted Grant to enter after, however what would his father think of him bringing home two girls!

Mr. Grenzer sat at the table with an empty gaze. His eyes had glossed over from his thoughts and the pen in his hand had dried up at the tip. She entered first, practically hustling around the foreign home before stumbling on the man she was told was her father. His gaze returned to him slowly as she approached. The distant face was not visible to him without his spectacles. All he saw was a toothy grin, glossy jet black hair, and the silhouette of a beautiful woman. He couldn't tell if the glimmer that came from her eyes was lust or hunger. It was a look he had seen before, years past.

"Irma?"

The name didn't drop like a declaration; it leaked into the stagnant air of the dining room like oil on calm water. Mr. Grenzer's hand began to tremble, a subtle, rhythmic shudder that started in his knuckles and traveled up his forearm. The dried-up pen slipped from his fingers, rolling across the scarred oak before clicking against the floorboards. He wasn't staring at a person. His clouded, unblinking eyes were locked onto a shadow from twenty-four years past. A hell he had tried to bury in his journal and quiet, solitary grief. Grant didn't just step back; his body reacted before his mind could formulate a thought. His hand shot out blindly, his fingers coiling around Manada's with a crushing, white-knuckled grip. She didn't flinch. She didn't pull away. Her presence stayed pinned to the floorboards beside him a cold, unyielding absolute reality amidst the sudden shift in the room.

But as Grant's eyes darted from his father's petrified face back to the girl in the doorway, the architecture of his mind began to fracture. The linear lines of the dining room warped. The edges of his vision bore a subtle, nauseating distortion, like heat rising off asphalt. Then he saw them. fractured, jagged phantoms that began to peel away from the plaster, their featureless forms twisting in agony. They didn't move with purpose; they fled in frantic, soundless panic, clawing at the air as they scrambled away from the girl standing at the threshold. The sight ignited every primal instinct in Grant. A suffocating dread flooded his veins, screaming at him to turn and run. Yet, his conscious mind, desperate to protect itself from the sheer, unadulterated terror of what he was witnessing, twisted the dread into something digestible. He pulled Manada in with suffocating strength to protect her from the ghosts that lunged, screaming soundlessly from every corner of his eyes. Then, he saw her. A fragile, innocent core of the storm that seemed to deter any hostility. He saw a gentle, long-lost sister trapped inside a nightmare, unscathed by the same unseen horrors that haunted him.

"Dad... are you alright," Grant gasped, his voice thick, uneven, and dripping with a strange, platonic desperation. He gestured toward Besra, his breath coming in shallow hitches. "She's... she's my sister. She's your daughter, Dad. Her health records were beneath yours." Mr. Grenzer didn't answer immediately. The silence stretched until the ticking of the kitchen clock sounded like iron striking iron. The wise man's gaze drifted between Manada's razor-sharp, watchful stance, and finally back to the unmoving girl standing under the crimson doorway light. Besra stood entirely still. She didn't blink. She didn't fidget. Her hands remained neatly folded over her womb, her expression locked in that bright, dead, customer-service smile that offered everything and felt like absolutely nothing.

Trapped between the visceral PTSD of his twelve months in the machine and the absurd, impossible narrative his son was spinning, Mr. Grenzer let out a dry, raspy breath. It was meant to be a chuckle, but it came out as a hollow, rattling cough. "A twin..." the old man muttered, shaking his head with agonizing slowness as a bead of cold sweat rolled down his temple. "Look at you, Grant. You step out into the cold for a few days and come dragging two women through my front door... as if this world just hands them out to anyone who asks." The joke hung in the air, heavy and rotting. Nobody laughed. Manada's posture didn't soften by a millimeter. Her dark, amber eyes stayed locked on Besra, her keen instincts humming with an intense, hyper-vigilant dread. She didn't need a census or a vision to know what was standing in front of them. She could feel the utter, rotten emptiness radiating off the girl. A thing that possessed nothing, no loyalty, and no capacity for human feeling. As the suffocating tension slowly settled into a numb, heavy exhaustion, Grant moved. He crossed the room, grabbing a thick, dark wool blanket from the back of the settee. His movements were slow, almost reverent, his mind completely idolatrous to the nature of the girl before him.

"Come on," Grant whispered softly, gently draping the heavy wool over her shoulders. "You're safe now. I've got you." He guided her down onto the small bench by the dying fire, sitting beside her and pulling her rigid frame tight into his chest. He curled his body around hers in a protective, desperate embrace. The physical touch was a quiet, agonizing horror for both women, unfolding in complete silence. For Besra, the sensation of human warmth pressing against her skin was revolting suffocating her in a cancerous intrusion that made her internal instinct scream. Yet, true to the compliance beaten into her, she didn't resist. She permitted his touch with the same flat, polite detachment she gave to every command, sitting like a painted doll in his arms. 

Across the dim room, Manada watched them, her jaw clenched so tight her teeth throbbed. Her stomach turned with visceral disgust as she watched the man she cared for pour genuine, tender devotion into an unfeeling husk that would happily watch him burn. Grant, completely oblivious to the silent agony around him, buried his face into the side of Besra's hair. In the dim crimson lights, Lindesay came rushing towards him. He saw her burst into flames, her body blackening, charring, and dissolving into ash. But as the vision burned itself out into cold embers, Grant felt no horror. In his logic, he interpreted the memory as a quiet blessing. a sacred handoff from the woman he lost to the sister he had just saved. He closed his eyes, holding the hollow girl tighter against the freeze of the night, while Manada stood silently, cautiously, alone in the shadows, watching the disaster take root.
